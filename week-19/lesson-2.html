📚 A Valley and a Conquest

Imagine being seven years old and sent away from your family to a school where you would go barefoot, sleep on a plain bed, and often feel hungry. Sparta did this to the sons of its citizens, and the rest of Greece watched with awe. Some Greeks admired it. Others were afraid of it. As you read, ask yourself what Sparta gained, what it cost, and who paid.

Sparta lay in a green valley in the south of Greece. A river called the Eurotas watered its fields, and the high Taygetus mountains rose behind it. The region around the city was called Laconia. On the other side of those mountains lay Messenia, a land with rich soil. Around 700 BC, the Spartans marched over the mountains and conquered it.

Most Greek cities that won a war took money or goods from the loser. Sparta did something harsher. It took the land and the people. The Messenians became state-owned farm workersHEL-utsThe conquered people of Messenia, who farmed for Sparta and were owned by the city. (helots). They farmed the land for the Spartans and handed over much of every harvest. The city, not any single Spartan, owned them.

The helots far outnumbered their masters. So Sparta lived with a constant fear that they would rise up. Many historians think that fear shaped nearly every rule you are about to read.

Think about what that meant. Most of the people living in the Spartan lands were people the Spartans ruled by force. The helots knew the land had once belonged to their own families. A Spartan could never fully relax. He had to be ready for trouble at home as well as trouble from abroad.

📚 Lycurgus and the Great Decree

Spartans said their way of life came from one lawgiver, Lycurgusly-KUR-gusThe lawgiver Sparta credited with its way of life. He is half legend. (Lykourgos). The writer Plutarch told his story hundreds of years later, and he admitted that nobody agreed on when Lycurgus lived. Today he is half legend. Plutarch says Lycurgus brought home a set of rules from the oracle at Delphi, a holy place where the god Apollo was said to speak. These rules were called the great decreegreat RAY-trahThe set of rules said to have given Sparta its government. (Great Rhetra).

Whether or not one man made them, the rules shaped Sparta for centuries. They set up a government and a way of life that the Spartans were proud to keep unchanged.

👤 FIGURE SPOTLIGHT · Lycurgus

Lycurgus is the lawgiver the Spartans credited with building their way of life. We know about him mostly from Plutarch, who wrote his biography long after Lycurgus would have lived. Plutarch tells us that Lycurgus traveled to other lands, studied their laws, and returned to Sparta at a time of disorder. He is said to have brought back the rules of the Great Rhetra with the approval of the god Apollo. Even Plutarch admits that the story has many versions. Some scholars think Lycurgus was a real man whose deeds were later enlarged. Others think the Spartans invented him to give their customs a famous founder. No portrait of him survives, and any statue or painting is only an artist's idea. What matters for history is that Spartans believed in him.

📚 Kings, Elders, and Overseers

Sparta had no single ruler. It had two kings at the same time, and they led the army in war. A council of eldersgeh-ROO-see-uhSparta's council of men over sixty, which helped the kings rule. (Gerousia) of twenty-eight men over sixty years old, together with the two kings, proposed the laws. The citizens met in the assemblyuh-SEM-bleeA meeting of citizens who gather to discuss and vote. and voted by shouting, and the louder side won. Each year the citizens also chose five overseersEF-orzFive officials, chosen each year, who watched over Sparta's rulers. (ephors) who kept watch over everyone, even the kings.

Later Greek writers admired this mix of one, a few, and many. Yet real power stayed with a small group of older, well-trained men. Historians call such a government an oligarchyOL-ih-gar-keeA government ruled by a small group of people., which means rule by a few.

Why build a government with so many parts? No one person or group could grab all the power. Each part could slow down the others. The kings led the army, but the overseers could watch them. The elders shaped the laws, but the assembly could say yes or no to them.

📚 Raised to Be Soldiers

The full citizens of Sparta were called full Spartan citizensspar-tee-AH-teezThe full citizens of Sparta, who trained for war. (Spartiates). A Spartiate did not farm or trade. His job was war. At age seven, a boy left home for the training systemah-go-GAYThe strict school that raised Spartan boys to be soldiers. (agoge). He lived with other boys, slept on simple beds, went barefoot, and was given little food. He learned to obey, to endure pain, and to speak in few words. When he grew up, he fought as a hopliteHOP-lyteA Greek citizen who fought as a heavily armed foot soldier. in the phalanxFAY-langksA tight block of soldiers who fought shoulder to shoulder in rows..

As an adult, he ate his meals in a common dining group. To stay a citizen, he had to keep paying his share of the food. A Spartan who could no longer pay lost his rights as a citizen.

Why was the school so harsh? A city that fears revolt needs soldiers who obey without question. The Spartans believed that hardship made strong men, and strong men made a safe city. Remember the phalanx from our last lesson. A line is only as strong as its weakest man. The training was meant to make sure that every man would hold his place. In Homer's poems, a hero fought for his own glory. A Spartan was taught to put the city first.

Free people who were not citizens lived in the towns around Sparta. They were called dwellers aroundpair-ee-OY-koyFree people in towns around Sparta who were not citizens. (perioikoi). They made tools and goods, traded, and fought beside the Spartans in the army. Because the helots farmed and the perioikoi made things, the Spartiates were free to train for war.

📚 Who Counted in Sparta

Only full Spartiates took part in the assembly. The perioikoi and the helots had no vote. The helots were kept down by force. Later Greek writers say that each year the overseers declared war on the helots, so that a Spartan who killed a helot would not be guilty of a crime. Historians argue about how often this happened, but the fear and the cruelty were real. Spartan women were not citizens in this political sense either, but they lived differently from most Greek women. They exercised, they could own land, and they ran family estates while the men were away.

Sparta was also a closed city. Strangers were sometimes sent away. The city used heavy iron bars as money. They were too bulky to carry far, which discouraged trade with other cities. This kept outside ideas and luxuries from creeping in. It also meant that Sparta added little to Greece in trade or new inventions.

The result was the finest army in Greece, but also a small citizen body and a large population living without a voice. Sparta had answered the question of how to defend a polisPOH-lisA city, its farmland, its citizens, and its gods, joined as one community.. It had not answered the question of who should share in ruling it.

You can think of Sparta as a club with a very small membership. The members had every right and every duty. Everyone else lived inside the club's walls but outside its door.

Now weigh it for yourself. The Spartans showed real courage, self-control, and loyalty to one another. Their boys learned to endure hardship and to put the city before themselves. Those are virtues. Yet this way of life rested on people who were owned and had no voice. Can a virtue be fully good when other people pay for it with their freedom? The Greeks argued about this, and you can too. Write your answer in your notebook with one reason.
