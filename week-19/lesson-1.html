📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why did Greece break into so many cities?

A people split by mountains and sea built a kind of community that the great empires never did. Today you will see how land, ideals, and weapons shaped the Greek city, and you will weigh two answers to an old question: what makes a person worth admiring?

📚 A Land Cut Into Pieces

Imagine being offered two lives. In one, you live long and quietly, and in a few generations nobody remembers your name. In the other, you die young, and people tell your story for thousands of years. In the poet Homer's story, a warrior named Achilles was offered exactly this choice. Keep it in mind as you read. Before you can see why the Greeks cared so much about it, you have to see the land they lived in.

Look at a map of Greece and you can see why it never became one country. Greece is a rocky peninsulapeh-NIN-suh-luhA piece of land with water on almost every side. with an enormous number of islands around it. Mountains run through the land like the bones of a giant. Between the ranges lie small valleys, and each valley has a little good farmland. To reach the next valley, a traveler had to climb a steep pass.

Compare this with Egypt and Mesopotamia. Those lands grew up on wide river plains. A strong ruler could march an army across flat ground and gather many towns under one crown. Persia, the great power at the end of our last story, ruled that way. Greece had no such plains. Each valley and island went its own way.

Yet the sea tied the Greeks together even as the mountains pulled them apart. A ship could cross the Aegean Sea faster than a farmer could cross a mountain range. Greeks became sailors and traders. Wherever they sailed, they carried their language, their gods, and their stories. Mountains divided the Greeks. The sea connected them.

      📚 A City, Its Farms, and Its Gods

      These small, separate communities became the Greek city-state. The Greeks called it a city-statePOH-lisA city, its farmland, its citizens, and its gods, joined as one community. (polis). A polis was more than a town. It had a city center, the farmland around it, the people who belonged to it, and the gods the people worshipped. Many were tiny, with only a few thousand people. There were many hundreds of them.

      A Greek did not say, "I am a citizen of Greece." He said, "I am a citizen of Athens," or of Corinth, or of Sparta. The Greeks shared a language, many gods, and great festivals. But they did not share one government. Greece was a world of many cities, and each city jealously guarded its own freedom.

      📚 Two Ways of Being Good

      The Greeks had two ideas about what makes a person admirable. The first comes from the poet Homer, who told of heroes like Achilles. Homer's heroes lived for contestAH-goneA struggle to win, in war, in sport, or in speech. (agon). They tried to show excellenceAIR-eh-tayBeing the very best at what you do, in courage and in skill. (arete) and win gloryKOO-dosFame and honor won by great deeds. (kudos). Remember Achilles and his choice. He picked the short life that would be remembered forever. Glory was the prize that made even death worth it.

      The Greeks loved contests even in peacetime. According to Greek tradition, the first Olympic Games were held at Olympia in 776 BC to honor Zeus. Runners, wrestlers, and chariot drivers came from many cities. Wars were paused for a sacred truce so that visitors could travel safely. The winner received a crown of olive leaves and went home a hero. The Olympics let everyone watch contest and excellence in action.

      🏛️ PLACE SPOTLIGHT · Olympia
      Photo: Ruins of Olympia, Greece, Stadium (Wikimedia Commons), CC BY 2.0.

      Olympia was not a city. It was a sacred place in the western Peloponnese, set aside for Zeus. Every four years, Greeks from many cities gathered here to compete. Visitors slept in tents and fields. The running track you see in the photo was a long strip of packed earth, and spectators watched from the grassy banks. A Greek from a faraway colony and a Greek from a mountain valley could stand side by side and cheer for the same runner. For a few days, the many cities of Greece felt like one people.

      📚 Hesiod and the Good Neighbor

      But a city cannot survive if every man chases only his own glory. Neighbors must trust each other, and farmers must be treated fairly. A second idea grew up beside the first. The poet Hesiod was a farmer who lived around 700 BC. He had a bitter quarrel with his own brother over land, and he wrote a poem called Works and Days about the hard life of a small farmer. Hesiod praised justiceDEE-kayFairness; giving each person what is owed. (dike). He believed that cities with fair judges and honest work would prosper, and that cities with crooked judges would suffer. Homer showed the brilliant hero. Hesiod showed the good neighbor. A strong polis needed both.

      📚 The Farmer-Soldiers

      Neighboring cities often fought over farmland. Around 700 BC, a new way of fighting spread through Greece. The new soldier was called a hopliteHOP-lyteA Greek citizen who fought as a heavily armed foot soldier.. He wore a bronze helmet and breastplate, carried a heavy round shield, and held a long spear. All of this armor cost a great deal of money. So hoplites were not only nobles. Many were farmers who owned enough land to pay for their own gear.

      Hoplites fought together in a phalanxFAY-langksA tight block of soldiers who fought shoulder to shoulder in rows., a tight block of men standing shoulder to shoulder, rows deep. Each man's shield covered his own left side and part of his neighbor's. That meant each man had to trust the man beside him. If one soldier ran, the whole line could break. Here was a new kind of glory. It did not belong to one hero like Achilles. It belonged to the whole line.

      Think about what that asks of a man. Charging ahead alone for glory takes courage. Standing in your place when you are afraid, because the man beside you is counting on you, may take more. A soldier who broke the line to chase glory could get his neighbors killed. Which kind of courage would you rather have? Write your answer in your notebook and give a reason.

      Often, men who fought for the city began to ask for a voice in running it. This did not happen the same way everywhere, and it was never guaranteed. But it explains why the farmer-soldier will appear again and again in this course.

      ⚔️ TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT · The Hoplite's Gear

      The full set of a hoplite's gear was called a panoply. It included a bronze helmet, a breastplate, bronze shin guards called greaves, a round shield about three feet across, a spear for thrusting, and a short sword as a backup. Bronze working was a skilled craft, so a full set was expensive. That is why only men with some wealth could buy one. The shield was the most important piece. It was held on the left arm by a central band and a grip near the rim. Because it covered part of the neighbor's side, a line of shields worked like a wall. A soldier alone in this armor was clumsy. A soldier in a line was dangerous. This is why a phalanx mattered more than any one hoplite.

      📚 Sailing Away

      Greek valleys had only so much good land. Between about 750 and 550 BC, the number of people grew, and farmland grew scarce. Some cities solved the problem by sending groups of settlers overseas. Each group founded a colonyKOL-uh-neeA new settlement far from home, started by people from an older city., a new polis in a distant place. Greek colonies rose in Sicily and southern Italy, along the shores of the Black Sea, in North Africa, and as far west as what is now southern France.

      A colony was usually independent. It had its own laws and its own citizens. It often kept ties of friendship and religion with the mother city. Through colonies, Greek ways of life spread around the Mediterranean Sea. Greek colonists in Italy and Sicily would one day meet a rising city called Rome.

      📚 Letters From the Sea

      Remember the Phoenicians, the sailing traders from our last story? Their ships brought more than goods. Around 800 BC, the Greeks borrowed Phoenician letters and made them into the Greek alphabetAL-fuh-betA set of letters, each standing for a sound, used to write a language.. The Greeks changed some letters to stand for vowel sounds, which the Phoenician letters did not show. The very word "alphabet" comes from the first two Greek letters, alpha and beta, which came from the Phoenician names for the first two letters in their list.

      With writing, a polis could post its laws for all to read. Poets like Homer and Hesiod could be written down and remembered. The same sea that carried Greek settlers had carried the tool that would keep their ideas alive.

      💪 KEEP THIS IN MIND

      Greece had no great plains and no single king, but it had something else: free communities held together by shared gods, fair judges, and neighbors who trusted each other with their lives. Notice that this strength was also a weakness, because a land of many cities could quarrel as easily as it could unite.
