    👤 FIGURE SPOTLIGHT · Cleisthenes

    Cleisthenes came from the Alcmaeonid family, one of the great noble families of Athens. His family had a complicated history. They had been exiled by the sons of Peisistratus and had worked to bring those tyrants down. After the fall of Hippias, Cleisthenes lost a power struggle with a rival named Isagoras, and then won it by turning to the Athenian people. Our main sources are Herodotus and Aristotle, who tell this story in somewhat different ways. We do not know exactly how Cleisthenes planned his reforms, or whether he foresaw their results. We do know that the city he left behind was organized differently from any before it. Little more is known about his life after 507 BC. The record goes quiet, but his work shaped Athens for generations.

    Cleisthenes Invents Democracy

    Part 1

    The End of the Tyrants

    Chapter 1The End of the Tyrants
    Chapter 1

    The End of the Tyrants

    In the center of Athens stood a statue of two friends who had killed a tyrant's brother. Athenians honored them for generations as the men who gave the city its freedom. Yet the tyrant they struck at lived four more years, and it was a Spartan army that finally drove him out. How does a city come to remember its own freedom? In this lesson you will see how freedom was really won, and who deserves the credit.

    After Peisistratus died in 527 BC, his son Hippias (Hippias) ruled Athens. At first things went on much as before. Then, in 514 BC, two men killed Hippias's brother. Hippias grew harsh and fearful. He began to punish Athenians he did not trust, and many fled. Those who had fled began to plan a return.

    These exiles found an unlikely helper. The priestess at Delphi, the holy place of the god Apollo, kept telling Spartan visitors to set Athens free. Sparta listened. A Spartan army marched to Athens, and in 510 BC Hippias was driven out. Notice the irony. Sparta had helped free Athens, but it was also helping to make a rival. This is a good example of how events can have results no one planned.

    Part 2

    A Quarrel Among Nobles

    Chapter 2A Quarrel Among Nobles
    Chapter 2

    A Quarrel Among Nobles

    Now the nobles fought for power. One was named Isagoras. His rival was Cleisthenes (Kleisthenes), from a famous family. When Cleisthenes was losing, he did something new. According to Herodotus, he brought the common people into his party, giving them a stake in the quarrel.

    Think about how bold that was. Nobles usually settled such fights among themselves. Cleisthenes broke the habit. He asked ordinary Athenians to help, and they said yes. Once the people had tasted that kind of power, they were not likely to give it back.

    Isagoras asked Sparta for help, and a Spartan king arrived with a small force. He drove out hundreds of families who supported Cleisthenes. But the Athenian council refused to obey. The people rose up and trapped the Spartans on the Acropolis. After two days, the Spartans left. Cleisthenes returned in triumph, and he now had the support of the people.

    Notice what the Athenians did here. They had just been freed from a tyrant, and now a foreign army stood in their city. They did not wait for their leaders to decide. They acted themselves. This is the first real sign of a people who see themselves as owners of their city.

    Part 3

    Cleisthenes Remakes Athens

    Chapter 3Cleisthenes Remakes Athens
    Chapter 3

    Cleisthenes Remakes Athens

    In 508 or 507 BC, Cleisthenes changed how Athens was organized. Before, a man's place depended on his family. Cleisthenes made it depend on where he lived. Athens and the land around it were divided into small local districts called demes (demoi). These were villages and neighborhoods where people knew each other. The demes were grouped into ten new tribes. Each tribe mixed people from the city, the coast, and the inland country. A noble family could no longer control a whole region.

    He also set up a new council of five hundred men. Fifty came from each tribe, and they were chosen by lot, which means by drawing names. The council prepared the business for the Assembly, where all citizens could speak and vote. Even the poorest citizens, the laborers, could take part in the Assembly. This was equal right to speak (isegoria).

    Cleisthenes added one more tool. Once a year, the citizens could vote on whether to send a powerful man away. This was ostracism (ostrakismos). A man who got the most votes had to leave for ten years. It was a way to stop any one man from growing too strong and becoming a tyrant again.

    Why did these changes matter? Think about the old way. A powerful noble could count on the men of his own region to follow him. After Cleisthenes, a man from the coast sat beside a man from the city and a man from the hills. They worked together in the same tribe. They learned to think of themselves as Athenians first. That shared loyalty helped to hold the city together.

    Democracy and Its Limits

    Chapter 4Democracy, and Its Limits

    Chapter 4

    Democracy, and Its Limits

    The Greek word for this new system was demokratia (demokratia), which means rule of the people. Herodotus wrote that once Athens was free of tyrants, it grew strong. Athenians fought harder for themselves than they ever had for a master.

    But this was not yet full democracy. The old Council of the Hill of Ares still held great power. Real change would wait for a reformer named Ephialtes in 462 BC. Even then, many people were left out. Only free men born in Athens counted as citizens. Women could not vote or hold office. Slaves were not citizens. Foreigners living in Athens were not citizens either, even if their families had lived there for years.

    This is a hard fact of Athenian history. Athenians created one of the first systems in which ordinary citizens helped to rule. They also kept most of the people who lived in their city out of it. Both parts of the story are true, and both parts are needed to understand Athens.

    Keep this balance in mind as you read on. A history that only praises Athens leaves out many people. A history that only blames Athens leaves out what was new and brave. A good student of history holds both thoughts.
