📚 Athens Before Solon

Last week a farmer named Hesiod warned that a city with crooked judges would suffer. About a century later, Athens was learning how right he was. Its law let a rich man take a poor man's own children as payment for a debt. Could a city like that last? Hold on to that question. It sets up this whole story.

Last week you met Sparta, a city that froze its citizens into a soldier class. Athens took a different road. But around 600 BC, Athens looked like many other Greek cities. It was ruled by a small group of rich families, the aristocracyair-ih-STOK-ruh-seeRule by a small group of rich families whose power passes from parent to child.. Each year, the aristocrats chose nine rulersAR-konsThe nine top officials of Athens, chosen each year. (archontes) from among themselves. When an archon's year ended, he joined the Council of the Hill of AresAIR-ee-OP-uh-gusA powerful council of former archons that watched over the laws. (Areopagus). This council watched over the laws and over the city's leaders. The best land and the highest offices belonged to a few families.

📚 The Debt Crisis

Most Athenians were small farmers, and a bad harvest could ruin one. A farmer who ran short of food borrowed from a rich neighbor. He promised his land as payment. Stone markers in the fields showed which land was owed to someone else. If he still could not pay, the lender could seize the farmer himself, and even his wife and children, as slaves. Some were sold in foreign lands.

Think about what this meant. Citizens of one city were becoming slaves of their own neighbors. A city cannot hold together like that. The poor were angry, and the rich were afraid. Athens was close to civil war. The story could have gone either way. A fight could have torn the city apart, or someone could find a fair answer.

Imagine being a farmer in this world. You work hard. You pay what you owe. Then one dry summer your crops fail, and everything you own is at risk. Your children could be taken from you. You would not feel like a citizen. You would feel like property. That feeling was spreading through Athens, and it was dangerous.

📚 Solon the Mediator

Around 594 BC, the Athenians turned to SolonSOH-lunThe Athenian lawgiver who settled the debt crisis around 594 BC. (Solon). He came from a respected family but was not rich. He was also a poet, and his poems told the Athenians what he thought of their quarrels. Both sides trusted him more than they trusted each other. He was chosen as archon and given the power to make new laws.

Solon's boldest act was to cancel the debts that were crushing the poor farmers. He also freed Athenians who had been made slaves for debt, and some were brought home from foreign lands. He forbade anyone from lending money on the promise of a man's own body. Later Athenians called this the shaking off of burdens.

But Solon did not do everything the poor wanted. Some of them asked him to divide the land of the rich and share it out equally. He refused. The rich were angry about the lost debts, and the poor were angry about the land. Solon said that he stood between the two sides like a boundary stone. He gave neither side all it wanted.

👤 FIGURE SPOTLIGHT · Solon

Solon was an Athenian poet, traveler, and lawgiver who lived around 600 BC. Our best information comes from Aristotle and Plutarch, who wrote long after his death, and from fragments of Solon's own poems. He was born into a good family but was not wealthy, which may be why both rich and poor were willing to trust him. After his reforms, he left Athens for ten years so that no one could pressure him to change his laws. Solon believed that a city fell apart when the rich grew greedy and the poor grew desperate. He tried to keep both groups within one community by giving each something and denying each something. Later Greeks counted him among the Seven Wise Men. His story is a reminder that a single fair-minded person, at the right moment, can change what a city becomes.

📚 Four Classes

Solon also sorted citizens into four classes by wealth, which was measured by the crops a family's land produced. The top class was the five-hundred-measure men, the largest landowners. Next came the horsemen, rich enough to serve in the cavalryKAV-ul-reeSoldiers who fight on horseback.. Third were the middle farmersZOO-jih-tyeFarmers rich enough to own a team of oxen; the class that supplied hoplites. (zeugitai), who owned a team of oxen and could pay for a hoplite's armor. Last were the laborersTHEE-teezThe poorest Athenian citizens, who worked for hire. (thetes), who worked for others.

The highest offices went to the top classes. According to Aristotle, the middle farmers could hold lower offices. The laborers held no offices, but they could attend the Assembly and sit on a jury that heard appeals. This mattered, because it gave the poorest citizens a small voice in running the city.

Notice what changed. Before Solon, birth decided who could rule. Now wealth did. A rich man whose family was not noble could rise. The old aristocracy was weakened, but it was not abolished.

Why did this matter so much? Think about a city where only a few families could ever lead. Now think about a city where a man could rise by the work of his farm. In the second city, more people had a reason to care about the laws. They had something to defend.

    📚 A Tyrant Arrives

    The peace did not last. Rival groups fought over power again. In 561 BC, a popular general named Peisistratuspy-SIS-truh-tusThe Athenian who seized power in 561 BC and ruled firmly from 546 to 527 BC. (Peisistratos) saw his chance. According to Herodotus, he wounded himself and his mules, rode into the city, and claimed his enemies had attacked him. The Athenians gave him a bodyguard. He used it to seize the Acropolis, the high rock at the center of Athens.

    He was driven out twice and returned twice. After 546 BC, he held power securely until his death in 527 BC. The Greeks called a man who ruled by force a tyrantTY-runtIn Greece, a man who took power by force. He was not always cruel. (tyrannos). The word did not always mean a cruel man. Peisistratus kept Solon's laws. He lent money to poor farmers. He built temples and started festivals that drew poorer citizens into public life. Many Athenians later remembered his rule as a mild one.

    Yet the lesson is plain. Solon's compromise had left many people unhappy, and an ambitious man could use that. When Peisistratus died, his sons took over, and the next lesson shows how that ended.

    The rule of Peisistratus teaches something. A tyrant can bring peace and still take away a people's freedom to decide. Some Athenians were pleased with his rule. Others quietly resented it.
