📚 The Delayer

After Cannae, Rome faced a hard choice. It could not beat Hannibal in open battle, and every attempt had ended in disaster. The man who saw this first was FabiusKWIN-tus FAY-bee-us MAX-ih-mus kunk-TAY-terThe Roman leader who wore Hannibal down by avoiding battle. (Quintus Fabius Maximus), whom the Senate had made dictator after the defeat at Lake Trasimene. His plan was simple: avoid a big fight. Instead, Fabius followed Hannibal at a safe distance, cut off his supplies, and picked off small groups of his men.

Many Romans hated this plan. It felt cowardly. They gave Fabius a nickname that meant "the Delayer," and they meant it as an insult. But after Cannae, they saw that he had been right. Hannibal could win any open fight, so Rome needed to deny him one. This patient strategy bought Rome the time it needed. Fabius' name later gave us the word "Fabian," meaning a strategy of slow, careful delay.

📚 The Allies Hold

Hannibal had counted on one thing. He hoped that after a few big defeats, Rome's allies would leave it, and Rome would collapse. Some did, including Capua. But most of the Latin and central Italian allies stayed loyal, even though Hannibal's army roamed through their lands for years.

Why? For two hundred years Rome had treated its allies as partners. They shared in the spoils and kept their own government. Hannibal, in contrast, was a foreign general with a small army far from home. This is the payoff of the system you studied last week. Rome could keep raising new legions, and Hannibal could not replace his men. He stayed in Italy for about fifteen years, but he never took Rome.

Think about how hard this was for ordinary Romans. They saw their fields burned and their sons killed, and they were told to wait. It took courage to stay calm and keep to the plan. That kind of patience was a virtue the Romans prized, even when it was unpopular. Fabius, an older man, was willing to be mocked if it saved the city.

📚 A Young Man in Spain

While Fabius delayed, another Roman was making a name. The Romans had also sent armies to Spain, and in 211 BC the two Roman commanders there, the father and the uncle of a young man named ScipioPUB-lee-us kor-NEEL-yus SIP-ee-oh af-rih-KAY-nusThe Roman general who beat Hannibal at Zama and won the name Africanus. (Publius Cornelius Scipio), were both killed. In 210 BC, when Scipio was only about twenty-five, the people sent him to Spain. It was an unusual choice, because he held no high office yet.

Scipio proved bold and clever. In 209 BC, he surprised and captured the Carthaginian capital in Spain in a single day. He drove the Carthaginians out of Spain within a few years. Meanwhile Hannibal's brother, also named Hasdrubal, marched with a second army to help him in Italy, but Roman forces destroyed him at the Metaurus River in 207 BC. Hannibal was alone.

📚 Carrying the War to Africa

Scipio then came up with a daring plan. If Hannibal would not leave Italy, he would strike Carthage itself. Many senators, including Fabius, were wary. They thought it too risky. Scipio won the argument, and in 204 BC he landed his army in North Africa.

Carthage panicked and called Hannibal home. In 202 BC, the two armies met at Zama, near Carthage. Hannibal had elephants and many new, inexperienced troops. Scipio had trained his men to open lanes so the elephants could run harmlessly through them. His ally Masinissamass-ih-NISS-uhThe Numidian king whose horsemen helped Scipio win. (Massinissa), king of the Numidians, brought fine horsemen who had switched to the Roman side. Roman cavalry broke Hannibal's horsemen, then came back and struck his army from behind. It was a kind of envelopmenten-VEL-up-mentSurrounding an enemy army on its sides and back., the same trick Hannibal had used at Cannae. Hannibal lost for the first time in a great battle. The victory gave Scipio a new name, Africanus.

    📚 The Peace and the Price

    The treatyTREE-teeA formal agreement between nations, often to end a war. was harsh. Carthage gave up Spain and its war fleet. It agreed to pay a large sum every year for fifty years. It could not make war without Rome's permission. Rome also took over Spain and ruled it as provincesPROV-insA land outside Italy ruled by Rome through an official sent from the city.. Carthage remained a city, but it was no longer a great power.

    Now look at what happened to the two powerful rulers. Hannibal stayed in Carthage and tried to fix its government. His enemies there accused him, and he went into exileEG-zylBeing forced to leave your home country. in 195 BC. He served other kings who feared Rome. At last, when the Romans were about to catch him, he took poison rather than be handed over. Scipio, for his part, returned in triumph. But rivals in the Senate envied his fame and accused him of taking bribes. He retired to his country estate and died there, bitter, about the same time as Hannibal.

    These endings carry a warning. Rome's old habit of sharing power depended on leaders being willing to stay among equals. A man who won too much glory could become a threat. Over the next century, the Republic would struggle with that very problem.

    💪 KEEP THIS IN MIND

    Rome won the Second Punic War through patience, loyal allies, and a bold young general. Carthage lost its empire, its fleet, and its power. Yet Rome's greatest heroes often ended badly, which shows how rivalry and jealousy were beginning to grow inside the Republic.
