📚 A Son's Oath

When he was a boy, his father made him swear never to be a friend of Rome. He kept that oath for the rest of his life. He led tens of thousands of men and a herd of elephants over the Alps in late autumn, and he arrived in Italy with only a fraction of them. The Romans, who thought they were safe, were stunned. What does it take to keep a promise made in childhood?

Carthage had lost the First Punic War, but it had not lost its pride. Hamilcar Barcahuh-MIL-kar BAR-kuhThe Carthaginian general who held out against Rome in Sicily and then built an empire in Spain. took an army to Spain and began to build a new base there. Spain had silver mines, and its silver paid for troops and pleased Carthage's leaders. It also supplied tough soldiers. Carthage still relied on mercenariesMER-suh-nair-eeA soldier who fights for pay and not for his own country., but now it had a core of Spanish troops who followed Barca loyally. (Hasdrubaal) ruled next, and when Hasdrubal died, the army chose Hamilcar's son as commander. He was twenty-six years old. His name was HannibalHAN-ih-bulThe Carthaginian general who invaded Italy. (Hannibaal).

Our sources say that when he was a boy, his father made him swear never to be a friend of Rome. We cannot be sure the story is exact, but Hannibal's whole life fits it. He was a brilliant leader, and the soldiers loved him. He ate what they ate and slept on the ground in his cloak. Hannibal had also learned from Greek teachers, and he understood how to read his enemies.

📚 The Spark at Saguntum

Spain became the cause of war. A city called Saguntum lay south of the river Ebro, where Carthage claimed rule. Rome had made friends with its people. Hannibal laid siege to it in 219 BC. It held out for about eight months and then fell. Rome by then ruled Sicily, Sardinia, and Corsica as provincesPROV-insA land outside Italy ruled by Rome through an official sent from the city. and was watching Spain closely. Rome demanded that Carthage hand Hannibal over, and Carthage refused. The Second Punic War began in 218 BC. Each side later blamed the other for starting it, and Romans told the story as though Hannibal had broken a treaty.

📚 The March

Hannibal faced a puzzle. Rome ruled the sea, so he could not sail an army to Italy. Instead, he decided to march. He left Spain with a large army, which included Spanish and African troops, cavalryKAV-ul-reeSoldiers who fight on horseback., and elephants. The Greek historian Polybius says he started with about ninety thousand foot soldiers and twelve thousand horsemen. Many were left behind to guard Spain.

The march was brutal. The army crossed the Pyrenees mountains and fought its way through Gaul. At the Rhone River, the soldiers built rafts and ferried the elephants across, which was no small feat. Then came the Alps. It was late autumn, and snow was already falling. The paths were narrow and icy. Local tribes rolled rocks onto the column from above. Horses and men slipped to their deaths.

In about fifteen days, the army crossed the mountains. Polybius reports that Hannibal arrived in Italy with only about twenty thousand foot soldiers and six thousand horsemen, and most of the elephants did not survive. But he was there. The Romans were stunned. They had never thought anyone could bring an army over the Alps.

📚 Trebia and Lake Trasimene

Hannibal knew he could not match Rome in numbers. He had to use his wits. At the Trebia River in December of 218 BC, he lured the Roman army across the icy water on a cold morning, before it had eaten. Then he sprang a trap with troops hidden behind them. The Romans were beaten badly.

The next spring, a Roman commander named Flaminius chased Hannibal through the hills of central Italy. Hannibal hid his army on the hills beside Lake Trasimene, in morning fog. When the Romans marched along the shore, he attacked them on three sides. Thousands were killed, including Flaminius. Two Roman armies were gone, and Rome had only a few trained troops left.

📚 Cannae

In 216 BC, Rome tried again. It raised an army of about eighty thousand, far larger than Hannibal's, and sent it to meet him near the village of Cannae. The Romans put their strongest troops in the center and planned to push straight through.

Hannibal set a trap. He placed weaker troops in the center and let them bend backward. As the Romans pressed forward, they were drawn into a pocket. Meanwhile his best infantry stood on the sides, and his cavalry beat the Roman horsemen and swung around behind. Soon the Romans were surrounded. Their numbers meant nothing, because they were packed too tightly to fight. Tens of thousands were killed, among them one of the two consuls. This kind of attack, which surrounds an enemy on both sides, is called a double envelopmenten-VEL-up-mentSurrounding an enemy army on its sides and back.. It is still studied in military schools.

It was Rome's worst defeat ever. Some of its allies in the south, including the rich city of Capua, switched sides.

📚 The Road Not Taken

After Cannae, Hannibal's cavalry commander Maharbalmuh-HAR-bulHannibal's cavalry commander at Cannae. (Maharbaal) is said to have urged him to march on Rome at once. According to Livy, Hannibal held back. Maharbal then replied that Hannibal knew how to win a victory but not how to use one.

Was Maharbal right? Hannibal had no siege machines, and Rome had strong walls. His army was small and far from home, and he needed help from Carthage that never came. But Rome was in shock, and it had few soldiers. Had Hannibal moved at once, the history of the world might have been very different. Historians still argue about it, and we cannot know the answer. What we do know is that Rome did not give up, and Hannibal was in the wrong place for a quick win. Next time, you will see how the Romans fought back.
