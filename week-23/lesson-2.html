📚 The Rock Above the City

Under Pericles, Athens began a great building program on the acropolisuh-KROP-uh-lisThe high rock at the center of Athens, crowned with temples.. The Persians had burned the old temples. Now Athens built new ones, grander than any before. The greatest was the Parthenon, a temple to the goddess Athena, begun in 447 BC. It was built of white marble and finished in just fifteen years or so. Inside stood a huge statue of Athena by the sculptor Phidias, made of gold and ivory.

Not everyone approved of the cost. Part of the money came from the tribute paid by Athens' allies. Plutarch reports that Pericles' enemies said Athens was decorating itself like a vain woman with other people's money. Pericles answered that the city had done the work of defending Greece, and so could spend the funds on its glory. This was a quarrel about how far a ruling city may use the wealth of those it rules.

Imagine walking up the Acropolis in those days. The marble shone in the sun. The temple stood above the whole city, and every Athenian could see it from home. It reminded them of Athena, their protector, and of their city's victory over Persia. It was also a message to other Greeks that Athens was great.

📚 A Festival for a God

Athens held many festivals, and the City City Dionysiady-uh-NISH-uhThe yearly spring festival of the god Dionysus, where plays were performed. (Dionysia) was among the grandest. Each spring, the city honored the god Dionysus with a procession and with plays. The plays were performed in the Theater of Dionysus, on the slope of the Acropolis. It held nearly twenty thousand people. Think of that: a large share of the citizens could gather in one place to watch a play.

The festival was a contest, like the Olympics you met earlier. Playwrights competed, and judges chosen by lot awarded prizes. The city also chose rich citizens to pay for the chorus. Such a person was called a play sponsorkoh-REE-gosA rich citizen who paid for a chorus as a public duty. (choregos). He paid for training and costumes, as a duty to the city. If his chorus won, he won glory, and so did the city. The Greek love of honor was thus put to public use. Private ambition fed the city's pride.

Notice how different this is from the Homeric hero you met in Lesson 19.1. There, glory belonged to one man. Here, a rich man's gift to the city won glory for him and for everyone. The old love of contest was now used to serve the community. This is a good example of how a city can guide its citizens' ambition.

📚 Tragedy and Hard Choices

Greek tragedyTRAJ-uh-deeA serious play about human suffering and hard choices. took stories from myth and made the audience face a hard question. AeschylusES-kuh-lusAn early Athenian writer of tragedies. (Aischylos) wrote The Persians in 472 BC, only a few years after Salamis. It is the oldest Greek play we still have, and it shows the war from the Persian side. SophoclesSOF-uh-kleezAn Athenian writer of tragedies, author of Antigone. (Sophokles) wrote the play Antigone. In it, Antigone buries her dead brother against the order of King Creon, who says the brother was a traitor. The play asks whether a person's duty to family can outweigh the law of the state. The audience was forced to think about it. No simple answer was offered.

The chorusKOR-usA group of performers who sing and dance together in a play. sang and danced and often spoke for the city itself. It reminded the audience that these choices affected a whole community.

These plays were not just entertainment. They were part of a religious festival, and they gave the whole city a chance to think about justice, duty, and the limits of human power together. A young man who watched Antigone would remember it when he later served on a jury or voted in the Assembly.

📚 Comedy and the Sophists

ComedyKOM-uh-deeA play meant to make people laugh, often by poking fun at real people and ideas. had a different job. Aristophanesair-ih-STOF-uh-neezThe Athenian writer of comedies who mocked politicians and thinkers. (Aristophanes) wrote funny plays that poked fun at real people, even the leaders of Athens. In 423 BC, he wrote The Clouds. It mocks the paid teachers of argumentSOF-istsTraveling teachers who charged fees to train young men in speaking and arguing. (sophistai), who charged fees to teach young men how to win arguments. It also made fun of a thinker named Socrates. Aristophanes was a conservative, and he feared that clever talk could make a weak argument seem strong. Think about that. A free city let a playwright make fun of its own leaders and thinkers in front of twenty thousand people.

📚 The First Historians

This was also the time of the first great historians. Herodotushih-ROD-uh-tusThe Greek writer who told the story of the Persian Wars. (Herodotos) wrote about the Persian Wars and the world around them. He collected stories from many places and often told them with wonder. He shows the Greeks and the Persians as people with their own reasons.

Thucydidesthoo-SID-ih-deezThe Athenian writer who recorded the war between Athens and Sparta. (Thoukydides) was a younger man, and he wrote about the war between Athens and Sparta. He wanted facts, and he looked for the causes behind them. He was stern where Herodotus was warm. Together they started a tradition of asking what happened, and why. They were rivals in method, and history gained from both.

You should notice what these writers gave us. Before them, the past was mostly told as myth or praise. After them, people began to check sources, compare stories, and search for causes. That habit is still the heart of history. When you study history in this course, you are following the road they began.
