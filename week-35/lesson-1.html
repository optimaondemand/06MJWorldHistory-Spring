📚 The Roman Peace

After Augustusaw-GUS-tusA title meaning 'the revered one,' given to Octavian in 27 BC., Rome had a long stretch of peace and order inside its borders. Historians call it the Pax Romanapax roh-MAH-nahThe long time of peace and order inside the Roman Empire. (Pax Romana). It lasted about two hundred years. There was fighting on the edges and now and then a civil war. But for most people in the empire, life was safer than it had been in a hundred years.

The peace was not always calm. Augustus's family, called the Julio-Claudians, ruled for almost a hundred years, and some of them were cruel. Nero, the last of them, died in AD 68. Next came the Year of the Four Emperors in AD 69, when four generals fought for the throne. The winner was Vespasian, a tough old soldier. Rome survived the crisis because the system had become deeper than any one man.

📚 Ruling Far Lands

By AD 117, when the emperor TrajanMAR-kus UL-pee-us tray-JAY-nusThe soldier-emperor under whom the empire grew to its greatest size. died, the empire was at its greatest size. It stretched from Britain in the northwest to Syria in the east, and from the Rhine and Danube rivers to the deserts of North Africa. Perhaps sixty million people lived within it.

How was such a place ruled? The empire was cut into provinces, each led by a governor sent from Rome. The governor kept order, heard court cases, and collected taxes. The emperor's own men watched the money. The provinces paid taxes, and in return Rome kept out invaders. Local people often ran their own towns. A city council in Gaul or Greece handled its own streets and festivals, and Rome did not interfere so long as taxes were paid and order was kept. This light touch was a secret of Rome's success.

Behind it all stood the army. The empire had about thirty legions, which was roughly 150,000 citizen-soldiers, plus other troops drawn from the provinces. They were stationed at the borders, not in cities. A soldier was paid in the denariusdih-NAR-ee-usThe main Roman silver coin., a silver coin used across the empire. Because a coin looked the same in Spain and Syria, trade moved easily.

📚 Roads, Water, and Stone

The Romans were fine builders. Their roads linked every province, and tens of thousands of miles were paved with stone. Soldiers marched on them, and so did merchants, messengers, and travelers. A messenger on the emperor's post could cover many miles a day by changing horses at stations along the road. Goods from far away came to the cities. Silks and spices came from Asia, and grain came from Egypt.

The Romans also built aqueductsAK-wuh-duktA channel or bridge-like structure that carries water from far away into a city., which carried fresh water many miles into the cities. They learned to make concreteKON-kreetA strong building material made by mixing lime, sand, stone, and water, which hardens like rock. with volcanic sand, which hardened even under water. With it they built great domes, such as the Pantheon in Rome, whose dome still stands. The Colosseum, a huge arena, opened in AD 80.

📚 Life in the City

In Rome, a million people lived in a crowded city. Most were poor. Rich Romans lived in houses with courtyards, but most families rented rooms in tall apartment buildings, which could burn or collapse. The government gave away grain to poor citizens so that they would not starve or riot. Baths were public places to wash, talk, and relax. For entertainment, crowds went to chariot races and to the arena, where gladiators fought. Rulers knew that bread and games kept the city calm.

Rome had grown rich, but a Republic's habits were fading. The city's ordinary people did not vote in great assemblies anymore. Compare this with what you learned of Rome in the early Republic, when citizens served in the army and had a say in who led them. In the empire, the old sense of shared duty was weaker.

In AD 79, Mount Vesuvius erupted and buried the town of Pompeii under ash. Diggers have found streets, shops, and even loaves of bread, which show how ordinary Romans lived.

📚 Law, Language, and Citizenship

One of Rome's greatest gifts was law. A Roman law was written down, and it applied to everyone in the same way. A person who owned property could expect courts to protect it. Over time, citizenshipSIT-ih-zun-shipFull membership in a country, with its rights and duties. was given to more and more people. In AD 212, nearly every free person in the empire became a citizen.

Language spread too. In the west, people learned Latin, and today Spanish, French, Italian, and Portuguese all come from it. In the east, Greek stayed the common tongue, a reminder of Alexander's world. The Roman poet VirgilPUB-lee-us ver-JIL-ee-us MAH-rohThe Roman poet who wrote the Aeneid. wrote the Aeneid, an epic about Rome's beginning, and Romans loved it.

📚 Good Rulers and a Warning

Five emperors in a row, from AD 96 to 180, are often called the Five Good Emperors. They included Trajan and Marcus AureliusMAR-kus aw-REEL-ee-usThe thoughtful emperor and philosopher who died in AD 180., who was also a philosopher. Each of the first four chose a skilled man as his heir and adopted him. This worked well. But Marcus Aurelius had a natural son, Commodus, and passed the throne to him. Commodus was a poor ruler. This is a reminder that the empire's good fortune was not guaranteed. It depended on good men, and good men are never certain.
