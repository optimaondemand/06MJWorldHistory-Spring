📚 The Edge at the Rhine

Rome's frontierfrun-TEERThe edge of a country or empire, where it meets other lands. ran for thousands of miles. In the northeast, it followed the Rhine and Danube rivers. Augustus wanted to push the border to the Elbe River, deep in Germany. In AD 9, that plan fell to pieces.

A German chief named Arminiusar-MIN-ee-usA German chief who served in the Roman army and then destroyed three Roman legions. had served in the Roman army and been made a Roman citizen. He knew how the Romans fought. The Germans had no single king, and their tribes often quarreled, but Arminius made them work together for a short time. He tricked the Roman governor VarusPUB-lee-us kwink-TIL-ee-us VAIR-usThe Roman governor who led the three legions into the ambush. into leading three legions through the dark, wet Teutoburg Forest. There, the Germans fell on them from the trees. In an ambushAM-bushA surprise attack from a hidden place. lasting several days, about fifteen thousand men were killed. Augustus is said to have cried out, "Varus, give me back my legions!"

Rome never tried to take Germany again. The Rhine and the Danube became the northern frontier, and a dozen Roman towns grew on their banks. Here is a question worth asking. If Arminius had failed, what might Germany have become? Instead, free German tribes would one day become the dangers that the empire most feared.

📚 Britain and the Wall

In AD 43, Emperor Claudius sent an army to conquer Britain. It took years, and the Britons fought hard. At one point a queen of the Iceni named Boudica led a bloody rebellionrih-BEL-yunAn open fight against the people in power.. Rome crushed it, and in time southern Britain became a quiet province with roads, towns, and baths. Roman soldiers built forts, and traders and settlers followed.

The Romans never took all the island. In the far north, the Caledonian tribes of what is now Scotland stayed free. The Roman historian Tacitus tells us that in about AD 83 a Caledonian chief named CalgacusKAL-guh-kusA Scottish chief who, in a speech written by Tacitus, spoke out against Rome. spoke to his men before a battle. In the speech, Calgacus says the Romans are robbers of the world, and that "they make a desert and call it peace." Tacitus was not there, and he almost certainly wrote the speech himself. But he was the son-in-law of the Roman general there, and he chose to put those words in an enemy's mouth. That should make us think.

Later Emperor Hadrian had a wall of stone built across the narrow part of northern Britain, which took years and many soldiers. Hadrian's Wall marked the edge of the empire for centuries.

📚 Jerusalem Burns

In the east, the Jews of Judea lived under Roman governors, and many hated paying taxes and being ruled by pagans. In AD 66 a rebellion broke out. Rome sent a general, Vespasian, and when he became emperor, his son TitusTY-tusThe Roman general, later emperor, who captured Jerusalem. finished the job.

In AD 70, after a long siege and terrible hunger, Titus's army broke into Jerusalem. The great Temple was burned. JosephusTY-tus FLAY-vee-us joh-SEE-fusA Jewish leader who joined the Romans and wrote about the war., a Jewish commander who had joined the Romans, saw it happen and wrote a history called The Jewish War. He says that Titus tried to save the Temple, and that a soldier set it on fire by accident. Some modern scholars doubt him. He wrote for Rome, so he had reason to blame it as little as he could. Many Jews were killed or sold into slavery, and the last fort of the rebels, at Masada, fell in AD 73. A great arch in Rome still shows Titus's triumph.

📚 Parthians, Persians, and the Silk Road

In the east, Rome faced Parthia, an empire of Persian-speaking horse archers who had destroyed Crassus. Trajan marched against them and took their capital in AD 116. But his gains could not be held, and his successor gave them up.

Parthia sat between Rome and the great empire of Han China. The Han emperors ruled China for about four hundred years, and their empire was nearly as large as Rome's. Silk and other goods passed from China along the Silk Road, and Parthian merchants kept a share of every sale. In AD 224, a new Persian dynasty, the Sasanians, overthrew the Parthians. They were stronger and more eager to make war on Rome.

📚 A Century of Crisis

After AD 235, the empire went through about fifty years of crisis. Generals made themselves emperor and fought one another, and many of those emperors were murdered. Armies spent their time fighting each other instead of guarding the frontier. That left the borders open to anyone who wished to cross them. A terrible plaguePLAYGA deadly disease that spreads quickly through a large number of people. swept through the empire. Farmers fled land that was being raided, and cities built walls, which Rome had not needed for centuries. Prices rose, coins lost their silver, and the borders were raided by Germans and Persians.

In AD 260, the Persian king Shapur captured the Roman emperor Valerian in battle. No Roman emperor had ever been taken alive. Valerian died in captivity. For Romans, this was a shock. The empire seemed ready to fall apart. That was the empire's lowest moment, and it was the start of Rome's long decline. Yet it did not break. Strong soldier-emperors pulled it back together by the late 200s, though it was a different empire than before.
