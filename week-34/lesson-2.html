📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How did Octavian win a whole empire and still say he had saved the Republic?

Octavian beat Antony, ended the last of the Greek kingdoms, and then did something clever. He gave up his power in public and kept all of it in fact.

📚 The War of Words

He called himself "first citizen." He kept the old offices, the old names, and the old costumes, and he told the Romans he had handed the state back to them. Yet there was no one left in Rome who could stop him. Was this the Republic saved, or the Republic buried with a smile? Your answer may depend on whether you think liberty or peace matters more.

After Philippi, Antony lived in the East, and Octavian kept order in the West. For ten years, they worked together and fought in secret. Antony lived in Alexandria with Cleopatra, and they had children. In 34 BC, Antony held a great ceremony in which he named Cleopatra's children rulers of eastern lands. He also called Cleopatra's son by Caesar the true son of Caesar.

That was an insult to OctavianGY-us ok-TAY-vee-usCaesar's great-nephew and adopted son, who later became Augustus., who had Caesar's name. He used it. He told Romans that Antony had turned his back on Rome, that he was a puppet of a foreign queen, and that he meant to move Rome's capital to Egypt. Much of this was propagandaprop-uh-GAN-duhWords or pictures used to push people toward one opinion, often by showing only one side.. Octavian also got hold of Antony's will, and read it aloud in Rome. It said Antony wanted to be buried in Alexandria. Romans were angry.

Notice what Octavian did. He declared war on Cleopatra, not on Antony. That made it look like a war against a foreign enemy and not a civil war. It was clever, and it worked.

📚 Actium

In 31 BC, the two fleets met in the water off Actium, on the west coast of Greece. Octavian's navy was led by his friend AgrippaMAR-kus vip-SAY-nee-us uh-GRIP-uhOctavian's boyhood friend and best general, who commanded his fleet at Actium., a very able admiral. Octavian's ships were smaller and faster. Agrippa had cut off the enemy's food by sea, and Antony and Cleopatra had many big ships, but the crews were sick, hungry, and short of men.

Before the battle was decided, Cleopatra's ships slipped through the line and sailed for Egypt. Antony followed her, leaving his men behind. The army on land gave up within days. We do not know why Cleopatra left. Some ancient writers say she lost her nerve. Others say it was a plan to save the treasure. The result was clear, though. One man's decision to follow the queen cost him an empire.

In 30 BC, Octavian marched into Alexandria. Antony took his own life, and soon so did Cleopatra. Egypt became a Roman provincePROV-insA land outside Italy that Rome ruled through a governor., and the last kingdom founded by one of Alexander's generals was gone. Octavian made Egypt his own property. No senator could even enter it without his leave. The grain of the Nile would feed Rome, and he would hold the key.

📚 A Republic in Name

Now Octavian was master of the whole Roman world. He had an army of about sixty legions, which he cut in half to about twenty-eight. He had a huge fortune and no rival. Yet he had seen what happened to Caesar.

So in 27 BC, Octavian stood before the Senate and gave up his special powers. He said he was handing the state back to the Senate and the people. The senators begged him to keep some of them, and he agreed, and the Senate gave him the name Augustusaw-GUS-tusA title meaning 'the revered one,' given to Octavian in 27 BC., meaning "revered." He was also given charge of the provinces where most of the army was stationed. This was the heart of the matter. The Senate looked after peaceful lands, and Augustus held the legions.

Augustus kept the old offices, the old names, and the old costumes. He called himself princepsPRIN-sepsThe title Augustus preferred. It means 'first citizen' and sounds modest., or "first citizen," which sounded humble. He was given the power of a tribune for life, which let him block any law. He held command of the armies. This system is called the principatePRIN-sih-paytThe system of government started by Augustus, in which one ruler led Rome while keeping the Republic's old offices.. It was a Republic in form and a monarchy in fact, and later rulers are called emperorsEM-per-urA ruler with supreme power over a large realm, often with a title that passes to an heir..

        📚 Augustus Tells His Own Story

        When Augustus grew old, he wrote a long list of what he had done. This is called the Res Gestaerayz GES-tie DEE-wee ow-GUS-teeThe list of his deeds that Augustus wrote near the end of his life., or Deeds of the Divine Augustus. It was carved on bronze pillars outside his tomb. Copies were put up across the empire, and one survives on the walls of a temple in what is now Turkey.

        In it, Augustus says that he gave the state back to the Senate and people. He says that he held no more power than the other officials, though he had more influence than all of them. He lists the games he paid for, the temples he built, and the gifts he gave to the people. He says he paid soldiers and poor citizens out of his own money. He does not mention the proscriptions.

        Here is a fair question. Should we believe him? He is not lying about the temples or the games. But a list of one's own deeds is not a neutral report. What does he leave out? And what is he trying to make readers think? Compare this to Caesar's own books on Gaul. Later writers, such as the historian Tacitus, painted a darker picture of Augustus.

        📚 The Quiet Change

        Augustus ruled for more than forty years. He made sure the army was paid and loyal. He set up a fire brigade and a police force for Rome. He rebuilt the city so well that he is said to have found it made of brick and left it made of marble. He gave Rome peace after a century of civil wars. Many people were grateful. Yet they had lost something too. Under the Republic, Romans argued, voted, and held office. Under Augustus, the offices were still filled, but one man made the real choices. Few noticed the change, and that was exactly what Augustus wanted.
