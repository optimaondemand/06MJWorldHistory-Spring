📚 The Heir

He was eighteen, often sick, and no soldier. He had one gift, which was patience, and one asset, which was a name. In a world of generals, he was the least likely man to win. Before this lesson ends, he and two other men will have published lists of Romans who could be killed by anyone. What happens when a patient and ambitious person is given a free hand?

When Caesar died, he left a will. It named his eighteen-year-old great-nephew, Gaius Octavius, as his adopteduh-DOPTTo take a child into your family as your own. son and heirAIRA person who is to receive another person's money, property, or title.. Octavian was not a strong man or a great soldier. He was often sick. He had a different talent, which was patience. He knew how to wait, how to make friends, and how to let others make mistakes. Those gifts would prove more useful than a sword. The young man was studying in Apollonia, in Greece, when he heard the news. His friends told him to stay away from Rome, where it would be dangerous. He went anyway.

In Rome, OctavianGY-us ok-TAY-vee-usCaesar's great-nephew and adopted son, who later became Augustus. (as historians call him) took Caesar's name and claimed Caesar's money. He promised to pay the money Caesar had left to each citizen. Caesar's veterans heard the name of their general and rallied to him. Caesar's name was worth more than gold. Soldiers who had served under Caesar would follow his son almost anywhere. Within months this boy had an army.

Meanwhile Mark AntonyMAR-kus an-TOH-nee-usCaesar's loyal friend and general, who was consul in 44 BC., the consul, held Caesar's papers and wanted to lead Caesar's party himself. He did not take Octavian seriously at first. That was a mistake, for the boy was shrewder than he looked.

📚 Strange Friends

At first the Senate used Octavian against Antony. Cicero, the great speaker, had given a series of fierce speeches against Antony. Cicero hoped the Senate could use the young man and then push him aside. He is reported to have said that the young man should be "praised, raised, and discarded." But in 43 BC Octavian made peace with Antony.

The two men met on a small island in a river near Bologna with a third, LepidusMAR-kus ay-MIL-ee-us LEP-ih-dusA general and Caesar's old ally, the weakest member of the Second Triumvirate., who had been Caesar's cavalry commander. Each man had something the others needed. Antony had experience and a large army. Lepidus had troops near Rome. Octavian had the name of Caesar. None of them trusted the others, but together they were stronger than the Senate. They agreed to share power for five years. Historians call this the Second Triumvirate. The first one, with Pompey, Caesar, and Crassus, had been a private deal. This one was made legal by a vote. Each man now held the power of a king and no one could check him.

📚 The Proscriptions

The three men wanted money for their armies and revenge on their enemies. So they published lists of names. These lists were called proscriptionsproh-SKRIP-shunA public list of enemies who could be killed by anyone, with their goods taken by the state.. Anyone on a list could be killed by anyone, and a reward was paid for the head. The killer got money, and the state took the dead man's property. To the Triumvirs, a list was a quick way to pay the army. A rich man's house and lands could be sold to buy loyalty.

The Greek writer Appian, who lived about 150 years after these events, tells terrible stories of the time. He says that slaves betrayed masters and sons betrayed fathers, and others hid in sewers or in the houses of friends. Appian also tells of loyal wives and slaves who risked their lives to save people on the lists. The number of the dead is uncertain, though ancient writers say there were hundreds of senators and many more wealthy men.

The most famous victim was Cicero. Antony wanted him dead because of the speeches. Cicero tried to flee, but was caught by soldiers in December of 43 BC. His head and hands were nailed up in the Forum, where he had once spoken. That was a message to every Roman who dared to speak freely.

📚 Philippi

Meanwhile BrutusMAR-kus JOO-nee-us BROO-tusThe senator, once Caesar's friend, who became the chief conspirator. and CassiusGY-us KASH-us lon-JY-nusA soldier and senator who helped plan Caesar's killing. had gathered a big army in the East. In 42 BC, the Triumvirs marched against them and met them at Philippi in Macedonia. The fighting was in two battles. In the first, Cassius killed himself after wrongly thinking his side had lost. In the second, Brutus was beaten and also took his own life. The battles were long and hard, and thousands of Romans died on both sides. Romans killing Romans had become the usual way of settling politics. With them died the last real hope of restoring the old republicree-PUB-likA country ruled by elected leaders and laws, not by a king. by force.

📚 Dividing the World

After Philippi, the Triumvirs shared out the Roman world. Antony took the rich East, including Greece and Asia. Octavian took the West, with the harder task of settling the veterans in Italy. Lepidus was given Africa, and soon he found that he counted for very little. Octavian's work was no easier. He had to find farms for tens of thousands of soldiers who wanted land, which meant taking it from Italian farmers. Many of those farmers lost their homes.

In 41 BC, Antony met the queen of Egypt, Cleopatra, at the city of Tarsus. He was in his forties and she was twenty-eight. They would shape each other's lives, and the ending of the Republic. The two halves of the Roman world now had two masters, and each man was watching the other.
