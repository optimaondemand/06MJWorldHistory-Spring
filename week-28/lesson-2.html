📚 Two Consuls and Their Helpers

After Rome threw out its kings, it needed leaders. Its answer was to make many offices, each with limits. The two highest were the consulsKON-sulOne of the two chief officials of the Roman Republic, elected for one year.. They were elected each year. They led the army and ran the government. But each consul could stop the other, so neither could act alone. Why make so many offices? The Romans feared that one man with too much power would act like a king. So they set short terms and shared the work among many hands. And after a year, the consul had to step down.

Other officials helped. Praetors served as judges. Censors counted the citizens and checked on their conduct. Quaestors handled public money. Rome also had a special office for dire emergencies. In a great danger, the Senate could name a dictatorDIK-tay-terIn Rome, a leader given total power for a short time during an emergency.. A dictator held full power, but only for six months. The Romans trusted this office because they believed good men would give up power when the danger passed. You will meet such a man next week.

📚 The Senate and the People

The SenateSEN-itThe council of Rome's leading men, who advised the government. was a council of about three hundred men, most of them former officials. It did not pass laws by itself, but its advice carried great weight. It guided foreign policy and controlled the treasury. Think about what this meant for a young Roman. He might serve as a soldier, then as a quaestor, and later as a praetor. If he did well, he might one day be elected consul. Each step tested his character, and the voters watched. Because senators served for life, they knew more about government than any one-year consul.

The people also had a voice. Citizens met in assemblies to elect officials and vote on laws. Their votes were counted by groups, and the groups of wealthy men voted first, so rich Romans had more influence. The tribunesTRIB-yoonAn official chosen to protect the common people of Rome., who had been won in the struggle between patricianspuh-TRISH-unA member of one of Rome's old, powerful families. and plebeianspluh-BEE-unA common citizen of Rome, such as a farmer or craftsman., could use a vetoVEE-tohThe power to stop a law or decision. It is Latin for "I forbid." to block an act of any official. In time, plebeians could hold nearly every office, even consul.

📚 A Greek Looks at Rome

A Greek named Polybiuspuh-LIB-ee-usThe Greek historian who wrote about how the Roman government worked. (Polybios) lived in Rome as a young man, around 167 BC. He had been taken there from Greece after Rome defeated Macedon. He watched the Romans and decided their government was a marvel. He thought it blended three forms. The consuls were like kings, the Senate was like an aristocracy, and the assemblies were like a democracy. Each part could check the others, and each needed the others to get anything done.

Compare this with the Greeks you have studied. Sparta also had two kings, a council of elders, and a group of magistrates. Athens gave power to all male citizens in the Assembly. The Greek thinker Aristotle sorted governments by who ruled and for whose good. Polybius believed Rome's mixture gave it more stability than any one kind alone. Many centuries later, the founders of the United States studied Rome closely. They also divided power among branches. Our Senate takes its name from Rome, and the president's veto does too.

📚 Custom Holds It Together

Laws were only part of what made Roman life work. Romans also honored the custom of the ancestorsmose my-YOR-umThe old customs that Romans believed every good citizen must follow. (mos maiorum). It was the sense that good Romans should do what their forefathers had done. Honor, duty, and loyalty were not written in any code, but a Roman who ignored them could be shamed.

Custom began at home. The head of each family was the father of the familypah-ter-fuh-MIL-ee-usThe oldest living man in a Roman family, who held power over the household. (paterfamilias). By law, he held great power over his wife, his children, and the slaves of his household. Custom, however, expected him to use that power with care and to consult the family.

Custom also tied rich and poor together. A powerful man was a patronpuh-TROH-nusA powerful Roman who protected and helped those who depended on him. (patronus). He gave advice, helped in court, and sometimes gave money or food to those who depended on him. In return, each of these clientsKLEE-enssA Roman who received help from a patron and gave loyalty in return. (cliens) gave him loyalty, and visited him early in the morning to show respect. Their votes and support helped the patron win office. These bonds crossed class lines, and they gave poor Romans someone to turn to.

📚 Who Counted?

Who actually took part in this government? Only citizens could vote and hold office, and only free adult men who were citizens could do both. Women who were born citizens had legal rights. They could own property in some cases, but they could not vote or hold office. Children belonged to their families. Slaves had no rights, and many foreigners were not citizens either. As in Athens, the share of the people who truly ruled was smaller than the whole population.

Even so, the Romans had found a way to share power more widely than most ancient states. A farmer could vote, serve as a soldier, and one day perhaps be elected. As Rome grew, many more people would ask to join the circle of citizens, and the question of who could belong would shake the Republic.

      💪 KEEP THIS IN MIND

      The Roman Republic worked because power was split among consuls, the Senate, and the people, and because Romans honored their ancestors' customs. But it relied on those customs more than on any written rule. When leaders stopped respecting them, the Republic would have little to fall back on.
