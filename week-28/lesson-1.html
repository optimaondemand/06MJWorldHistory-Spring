📚 A City on Seven Hills

A noblewoman named Lucretia is said to have told her family what a prince had done to her. A nobleman named Brutus then drew a knife and swore an oath to drive out the king. That is the Romans' own story of how their free state began. Whether or not every detail is true, the Romans believed it, and it shaped what they thought a Roman owed to Rome.

Rome did not begin as a great power. Italy is a long peninsula shaped like a boot, with a spine of mountains down the middle. In the middle of the peninsula, a river called the Tiber winds toward the sea. Not far up the river, a cluster of low hills rose above the marshes. That was the site of Rome.

The place had good points. The hills were easy to defend. The river brought boats, and the roads along it brought traders who carried salt and goods. Rome sat where several peoples met. The Latins, who spoke the language that became Latin, lived around the city. The Etruscans lived to the north, and they were skilled builders and metalworkers. Greek colonies stood in the south. In the first half of this course you met Rome and Carthage as two young cities. Now you will see how Rome grew into something new.

📚 Stories of the Founding

The Romans told stories about their beginning. In one story, a Trojan prince named Aeneas escaped from the burning city of Troy and sailed to Italy. In another, twin brothers named Romulus and Remus were raised by a wolf. Romulus built the city and became its first king. The Romans dated this to 753 BC.

How much of this is true? We cannot be sure. LivyTY-tus LIV-ee-usThe Roman writer who told the story of Rome's early years. (Titus Livius) told these tales about seven hundred years after the events, and he used old stories that had passed from person to person. Diggers have found huts on the Roman hills from about that time, so a small village really did exist. Livy's stories may not be exact history, but they tell us what the Romans believed about themselves. They believed they were tough, loyal, and proud.

📚 The Last King

For a time, kings ruled Rome. Some of them came from the Etruscans. The last was Tarquin the Proudtar-KWIN-ee-us soo-PER-busThe last king of Rome. (Tarquinius Superbus). The Romans remembered him as cruel and greedy.

Livy tells how the end came. The king's son wronged a noblewoman named Lucretia. She told her father and her husband and made them promise to take revenge. Then she ended her own life. A nobleman named Lucius Junius Brutus took up the knife and swore an oath. He and the others would drive out the Tarquins and never again put up with a king. The Romans rose up, and the king fled in 509 BC.

Notice what happened next. The Romans did not simply find a new king. They chose two leaders called consuls, and each served for only one year. The state was now a republicrih-PUB-likA government in which citizens choose their leaders and no king rules.. The Romans called it the res publica, which means "the public thing." It belonged to all its citizens. This was not an easy choice. Other cities in Italy kept their kings, and Rome could have done the same. The new government also had to survive threats from the Etruscans, who tried to bring the Tarquins back.

📚 Patricians and Plebeians

The new republic still had a deep problem. Two groups of Romans did not share power fairly. The patricianspuh-TRISH-unA member of one of Rome's old, powerful families. came from the oldest families. They held the high offices and the priesthoods. The plebeianspluh-BEE-unA common citizen of Rome, such as a farmer or craftsman. were the great majority. They were farmers, craftsmen, and shopkeepers, and they fought in the army. Yet at first they could not hold the chief offices. Many were also in debt, and a debtor could be seized and held as a slave.

In 494 BC, the plebeians took a bold step. They marched out of the city and refused to fight or work. This is called a secession. Rome needed their labor and their spears, and the patricians had to listen. The two sides agreed that the plebeians could choose their own officials, called tribunesTRIB-yoonAn official chosen to protect the common people of Rome.. A tribune's task was to protect any plebeian from unfair treatment by an official.

That was only the start. For about two hundred years, plebeians pressed for more rights, and patricians gave way a little at a time. This struggle is called the Conflict of the Orders. Notice that it did not become a civil war. Both sides bargained, and both sides gave something up. Historians who admire the Roman Republic often point to this habit of compromise as a main source of its strength.

    📚 Laws for Everyone

    One of the plebeians' complaints was that the laws were secret. Only patrician judges knew them, and they could bend them as they liked. About 450 BC, the Romans wrote their laws down. They carved them on bronze or wooden tablets and set them up in the Forum, the open square at the heart of the city. These laws were called the Twelve Tablesdoo-oh-DESS-im TAB-yoo-lyRome's first written laws, carved and put on public display. (Duodecim Tabulae).

    The laws were short and sometimes harsh, and they dealt with family, property, debt, and crime. But their greatest value was that everyone could now read them. A law that is written and public is harder for a powerful person to twist. This idea, that laws should be known and should bind everyone, is called the rule of law. It became one of Rome's lasting gifts to the world. Roman boys later memorized the Twelve Tables in school.

    Why did all of this matter? Rome had no army marching on its own people and no single ruler to command peace. What held the city together was a set of habits: take turns in office, listen to the other side, and obey the law. Those habits were a kind of virtue. Next, you will see how the Romans built them into a government.
