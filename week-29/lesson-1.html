📚 The Farmer Who Saved Rome

A farmer is plowing his field when messengers arrive and tell him that Rome has made him its ruler. He does not celebrate. He is probably thinking about his harvest. Two weeks later, he hands back all his power and goes home. Why would the Romans tell that story for centuries?

Rome's first wars were fought against close neighbors, and they were often a matter of life and death. One story shows what the Romans most admired. Around 458 BC, an enemy army trapped a Roman force in the hills. The SenateSEN-itThe council of Rome's leading men, who advised the government. needed a leader, so it chose a dictatorDIK-tay-terIn Rome, a leader given total power for a short time during an emergency.. The man it chose was Cincinnatussin-sin-NAH-tusA Roman farmer who twice served as dictator and then went home to his plow. (Lucius Quinctius Cincinnatus), and he was plowing his small farm.

Livy tells us that he wiped the dust from his hands, took command, and rescued the army in about two weeks. Then he gave up his power and went back to his farm. The story may have grown with the telling, but the Romans loved it. It showed the kind of citizen they honored: one who served the state and did not cling to power. You will read about him this week.

📚 The Army of Citizens

Rome's army was not a band of hired fighters. It was made up of citizens who owned land. They bought their own weapons, served in the spring and summer, and went home to farm. Because they fought for their own farms, they fought hard. A farmer who lost a battle might lose his home, so he had every reason to win.

Early on, Roman soldiers fought in a tight block like the Greek phalanx. Later, the Romans changed. The basic unit became the legionLEE-junThe main unit of the Roman army, with thousands of soldiers., with several thousand men. Each legion was split into small units called maniplesMAN-ih-pulzSmall groups of Roman soldiers that could move and fight on their own. (manipuli), which stood in rows with gaps, like the squares on a checkerboard. Soldiers first threw heavy javelins, then drew short swords and moved in close. A maniple could move on its own, so a legion could handle rough ground better than a phalanx. If one line was pushed back, the next stepped forward.

Discipline mattered as much as weapons. Roman officers punished cowardice harshly. When the march ended each day, the soldiers made a ditch and a wall for their camp. They did this even for a stay of a single night. Few armies took such care.

📚 The Gauls Sack Rome

Even Rome's army could fail. About 390 BC, a Gallic army from the north came down into Italy. The Gauls were tall, fierce warriors who lived north of the Alps. They beat the Romans at the Allia River and entered the city. They burned much of it. The Romans held out on one hill, the Capitoline, until they paid a heavy sum of gold to make the Gauls leave.

It was a disaster, and it could have been the end of the city. No one could have said for certain that Rome would recover. Instead, the Romans rebuilt, put up strong walls, and trained harder. They never forgot the shock. For a long time afterward, Romans feared the Gauls more than any other people.

📚 Allies, Not Subjects

Over the next century, Rome fought many wars against its neighbors. The Latins lived nearby. The Samnites, tough hill fighters, fought Rome in three long wars. In one, a Roman army was trapped in a mountain pass and had to surrender. Rome did not quit. It came back and won.

After a victory, Rome did something unusual. It did not destroy the losers, and it did not rule them harshly. Instead, many of them became allies. Some received citizenship or part of it. Each ally kept its own government, but had to send soldiers to Rome's wars. Rome asked for troops instead of tribute, and so each victory made Rome's army larger. Even defeated enemies had something to lose if Rome fell, and some of them even hoped to share in the spoils.

Why would a defeated city accept this? It kept its lands, its laws, and its pride, and it could hope to share in later victories. Rome's allies were not always happy, but most of them stayed loyal for generations.

Rome also built coloniesKOL-uh-neeA settlement of citizens in a new land, still tied to the home city., towns of Roman citizens planted on conquered land. And it built roads. The Appian Way, begun in 312 BC, ran from Rome toward the south. Soldiers could march quickly along it. Together, the colonies, the roads, and the alliances held Italy in a web that was very hard to break.

🎯 TRY IT: After the Victory

What happened: Rome usually spared the losers and did not rule them harshly. Many became allies, and Rome took soldiers from them in place of tribute. That made its army bigger with every win.
📚 A Victory That Cost Too Much

In the south of Italy, Greek cities such as Tarentum grew nervous about Rome's growth. They asked for help from PyrrhusPIR-usThe king of Epirus who fought Rome in southern Italy. (Pyrrhos), the king of Epirus, a restless general who admired Alexander. He crossed the sea in 280 BC with an army and elephants. Romans had never seen elephants in war.

Pyrrhus beat the Romans in two battles. But each time he lost many of his best men, and Rome simply raised more troops from its allies and fought on. He could not replace his losses. One story says he remarked that another such victory would ruin him. That is why a win that costs too much is called a Pyrrhic victory. By 275 BC, Pyrrhus gave up and went home. The Greek cities of the south came under Rome.
