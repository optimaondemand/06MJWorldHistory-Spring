📚 Diocletian Rebuilds

You began this course with the first cities. You have watched kings, citizens, and emperors try to solve the same hard problems of order and defense. Now you stand at the end of the ancient Roman world. As you read about Constantine, ask what was kept, what was lost, and what was handed on. You are one of the people it was handed to.

After the long crisis, DiocletianGY-us aw-REEL-ee-us vuh-LEER-ee-us dy-oh-KLEE-shunThe emperor who ended the third-century crisis and began the Great Persecution. became emperor in AD 284. He was a soldier from a poor family and a hard worker. He saw that the empire was too big for one man. He also acted like a god-king, and people had to kneel before him. So he split it. He took the East himself and gave the West to a partner. Later each of those two ruled with a junior emperor, which is called the tetrarchyTET-rar-keeRule by four men. Diocletian split the empire among four rulers.. The four worked as a team and shared the same goal, which was to keep the empire safe.

It worked for a time. Frontiers were strengthened, taxes were set, and prices were controlled, though that did not work well. But Diocletian also wanted the old gods honored. He feared that Christians would anger the gods. In AD 303 he began the Great Persecution. Churches were torn down, holy books were burned, and many Christians were killed. It was the worst of the persecutions. It failed, as the others had. In AD 305, Diocletian retired to a palace on the Adriatic coast.

📚 Constantine and the Bridge

When Diocletian left, the system fell apart. His mother, Helena, was a devout Christian in later life. Soldiers in Britain declared ConstantineFLAY-vee-us vuh-LEER-ee-us kon-stan-TY-nusThe emperor who ended the persecutions and favored the Christians. emperor in AD 306 after his father died. Over years of fighting, he beat his rivals. The big test came in AD 312. His enemy Maxentiusmak-SEN-shusConstantine's rival, who ruled Rome and died at the Milvian Bridge. held Rome, and Constantine marched on the city.

Two ancient writers tell what happened next, and they do not agree. One says that Constantine had a dream the night before the battle, telling him to paint a sacred Christian sign on his soldiers' shields. The other says he saw a cross of light in the sky. In either story, Constantine believed the Christian God gave him the win. At the Milvian Bridge, he won, and Maxentius drowned in the river. Constantine entered Rome as master of the West.

📚 The Edict of Milan

In AD 313, Constantine and the eastern emperor Licinius agreed to end the persecution. Their agreement is known as the Edict of Milan, though some scholars say it was a letter and not a true edictEE-diktA public order issued by a ruler.. It said that everyone, Christian or not, could follow the religion they chose. Property taken from the Christians was returned. Churches that had been shut were opened again, and Christians could meet in the open.

Constantine then went further. He gave money to build great churches, excused Christian leaders from certain taxes, and made Sunday a day of rest. Yet he did not make everyone Christian. Old temples stayed open, and he kept the title of the chief priest of the old religion. He was baptized only as he lay dying in AD 337. Did he truly believe, or did he want a faith that would unite his empire? The sources do not let us be sure, and probably the answer was both.

📚 The Council of Nicaea

Christians were not always agreed. A priest named AriusAIR-ee-usA priest from Alexandria whose teaching about Christ began the argument at Nicaea. taught that Christ was a created being and was less than God the Father. Most bishops disagreed. The quarrel was splitting the church. In AD 325, Constantine called about three hundred bishopsBISH-upA senior leader of the Christian church who oversees the churches of a region. to a council at Nicaea. They agreed on a creedKREEDA short statement of what a group of people believe., a statement of belief, which is still used by many churches. Notice that the emperor presided over a church council. The state and the church were now bound together.

In AD 330, Constantine dedicated a new capital at the old Greek city of Byzantium, now called Constantinople, or "Constantine's city." It sat on a strait between two seas and was easy to defend. It was also closer to the rich eastern provinces and the Persian frontier. Constantine gave it walls, a harbor, and a great track for chariot races. For a thousand years, it would be a great city.

📚 Far Beyond Rome

The Christian faith was moving beyond the empire as well. In the 330s, King Ezanaeh-ZAH-nahThe king of Aksum who made Christianity his kingdom's faith. of Aksum, in what is now Ethiopia, became a Christian. His coins changed from the old symbols to the cross. Around the middle of the 300s, Aksum's armies invaded Kush, an old kingdom already weakened for many years.

📚 What Changed and What Did Not

Constantine changed a great deal. Christianity went from a hunted faith to a favored one, and bishops now had rank, wealth, and the ear of the emperor. But the empire was still a rule of one man, with an army and a tax collector. It was still Rome.

Look back over the whole course. You began with the first cities, and you have watched kings, citizens, and emperors try to solve the same hard problems of order and defense. After Constantine, the West slowly lost its grip. The empire was divided, Germanic peoples moved in, and the last Western emperor was removed in AD 476. The East lived on for a thousand years. What Rome left behind, its law, its roads, and its Christian faith, would shape everything that followed.
