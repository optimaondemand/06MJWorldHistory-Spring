📚 Greeks Under the Persian King

You have already seen that Cyrus built the largest empire the world had seen. Among the lands he took were the cities of Ionian Greekseye-OH-nee-unzGreeks who lived on the coast of Asia Minor. (Iones), on the coast of Asia Minor, now in modern Turkey. These Greeks spoke the same language as the people of Athens and shared many of their gods. But they paid taxes to a Persian king and obeyed a Persian governor. In many cities, Persia backed a local tyrantTY-runtIn Greece, a man who took power by force. He was not always cruel. to keep order.

For a time the Ionians put up with this. Their cities were rich, and trade across the empire was good for business. But Greeks prized freedom, and some Ionians looked across the Aegean Sea and saw cities that governed themselves.

Picture a young Ionian merchant in Miletus. He is rich. He sails to Egypt and to Lydia to trade. Yet when he walks through his own city, he knows that the Persian governor can overrule his city's leaders. He cannot vote on the taxes he pays. Across the sea, men in Athens decide such things for themselves. It is easy to see why some Ionians felt a deep unease.

📚 Aristagoras Starts a Revolt

The spark came from AristagorasAIR-ih-STAG-or-usThe Greek ruler of Miletus who started the revolt in 499 BC. (Aristagoras), the ruler of Miletus, one of the greatest Ionian cities. He had promised the Persians he could capture the island of Naxos. The attack failed, and he feared the king's anger. Herodotus says he decided that a revoltree-VOLTA rising up against the people who rule you. was safer than facing Persian punishment. In 499 BC, he gave up his tyranny and called on the Ionian cities to rise against Persia.

Most of them joined. Aristagoras then went across the sea to find help. He asked Sparta first. The Spartan king heard that Persia lay three months' march from the sea and refused. Aristagoras then went to Athens. The Athenian Assembly, which had been working for just a few years under Cleisthenes' system, said yes. Athens sent twenty ships. The city of Eretria, on the island of Euboea, sent five.

Notice that the revolt began from fear and ambition, not from a grand plan. Aristagoras was thinking about his own safety. Yet his choice set a great chain of events in motion. Small decisions made by a few men can change the lives of millions. This is why historians study the choices of individuals, and not only large forces.

📚 The Burning of Sardis

The alliesAL-eyeA friend or nation that joins you in a struggle. sailed across the Aegean and joined the Ionians. They marched inland to Sardis, the Persian capital of that region. They took the lower city, and then a fire started. According to Herodotus, a soldier set fire to one house, and because most houses were made of reeds, the whole city burned. Among the buildings lost was a temple of a local goddess.

The Persians never forgot this. Herodotus tells a story that shows how deeply the news struck the Persian king. When Dariusduh-RY-usThe king of Persia from 522 to 486 BC. (Dareios) heard that the Athenians had joined the revolt, he asked who they were. Then he shot an arrow into the sky and prayed for revenge. He ordered a servant to say to him three times at every dinner, Master, remember the Athenians.

Athens had not meant to start a long war with Persia. Its ships went home soon after Sardis, and the Athenians took no further part in the revolt. But Darius did not see it that way. To him, Athens had joined his enemies and had burned a city of his empire. That was enough.

👥 PEOPLES SPOTLIGHT · The Ionian Greeks

The Ionianseye-OH-nee-unzGreeks who lived on the coast of Asia Minor. were Greeks who had settled the central coast of Asia Minor and nearby islands long before the revolt. Their cities, such as Miletus and Ephesus, were among the richest in the Greek world. Ionian thinkers were some of the first Greeks to ask what the world is made of. They shared a language and gods with the Greeks across the sea, but they lived inside the Persian empire. This put them in a hard spot. They had the culture of free Greek cities and the obligations of Persian subjects. Their revolt came out of that tension. Because they sat between Greece and Persia, the Ionians were the link that drew the two worlds into war.

📚 Persia Strikes Back

Persia took years to answer, but it did. The revolt spread for several years, and then the king's forces closed in. In 494 BC, near the island of Lade, a Persian fleet met the Ionian fleet. Some of the Ionian ships sailed away without fighting, and the Greek line fell apart. The Persians won. They then destroyed Miletus, the heart of the revolt. Its men were killed or marched away, and its people were scattered.

By then the Athenians and Eretrians had long gone home. They had played only a small part, and they had done the great king real harm. Darius knew it. A few years later, in 490 BC, a Persian fleet crossed the Aegean, island by island. It attacked Eretria and destroyed it. Then the fleet turned toward Athens.

📚 A Great King's Answer

Why did Darius come across the sea? The Persian king wanted to punish the cities that had burned Sardis. He also wanted to make sure that no Greek city would ever again help his subjects rebel. Some historians add another reason: a powerful king rarely forgave an insult. But we should remember that Darius could not see the future. He expected a quick victory. A force that had crushed an empire's rebels would, he thought, easily overcome one small city.

That expectation would be tested on the plain of Marathon, which is the subject of the next lesson.

Think about how the two sides saw the war. For Persia, it was a matter of order and punishment. For the Greeks, it was a matter of freedom. Both sides believed they were right. Understanding this helps you see why the war that followed was so fierce.
