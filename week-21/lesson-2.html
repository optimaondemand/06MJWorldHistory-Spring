📚 The Landing at Marathon

Stand on the shore at Marathon. Thousands of Persians fill the plain, and the men beside you are not soldiers by trade. They are farmers and craftsmen who are your neighbors. Your home is twenty-six miles behind you. The Persians have already burned Eretria. Do you march out to fight, or wait behind the walls? Ten Athenian generals could not agree, and the answer would decide everything.

In the late summer of 490 BC, a Persian fleet landed on the plain of Marathon on the coast of Attica, about twenty-six miles from Athens. With it came the exiled tyrant Hippias, who hoped to be put back in power. The Persians chose Marathon because its flat, open plain suited their horsemen. They believed Athens would soon fall.

The Athenians had no great army to call on. They had perhaps nine or ten thousand hoplitesHOP-lyteA Greek citizen who fought as a heavily armed foot soldier., and a small band of friends from the little city of Plataea. They also had a problem. Their leaders were split. Some wanted to wait inside the walls. Others wanted to march out and fight.

Think about the Athenians on that shore. They could see the enemy's camp on the plain. They knew what had happened to Eretria a few days before. The Persians had burned its temples and marched its people away. If Athens lost, the same would happen to Athens. Every man in the army knew what was at stake. Their homes, their families, and their freedom were all on the line.

📚 The Vote

Athens had ten generals, and the vote was tied. The decider was the war leaderPOL-uh-markThe Athenian official in charge of the army. (polemarchos), a man named Callimachuskuh-LIM-uh-kusThe Athenian war leader who cast the deciding vote to fight. (Kallimachos). According to Herodotus, the general Miltiadesmil-TY-uh-deezThe Athenian general who led the plan for the battle of Marathon. (Miltiades) urged him to vote to fight. Miltiades had once served in the Persian empire and knew how Persians fought. Callimachus voted for battle. It was a turning point. A single vote may have decided the fate of Athens.

Meanwhile Athens sent a runner named Pheidippidesfy-DIP-ih-deezThe runner Athens sent to ask Sparta for help. (Pheidippides) to ask Sparta for help. Sparta agreed, but said the Spartans could not march until the moon was full, because of a religious festival. The Spartans arrived only after the battle was over. Here is another place where history turns on a small thing. If the festival had fallen on a different date, Sparta might have fought beside Athens.

A vote like this is easy to forget. We remember battles, but we often forget the quiet decisions that come before them. Here, one man's vote may have decided whether Athens fought or waited. If Athens had waited, the Persians might have found friends inside the city who wanted to let Hippias return. We cannot know what would have happened, but we can see how close the choice was.

⚔️ BATTLE SPOTLIGHT · Marathon, 490 BC

Marathon is a flat coastal plain, a little over a mile wide, with marshes at one end and mountains behind it. The Greeks camped at the foot of the hills, where Persian horsemen could not easily reach them. The Persians kept their ships on the beach at their backs. Our main source is Herodotus, who wrote about forty years after the battle. He gives the numbers of the dead on each side, but no map survives, and historians disagree about exactly where the two armies stood. A mound near the plain is traditionally said to cover the Athenian dead. Whatever the exact details, the Greeks had chosen their ground well, and they struck before the Persians were ready.

📚 The Charge

The Greeks lined up as a phalanxFAY-langksA tight block of soldiers who fought shoulder to shoulder in rows. and advanced. They moved fast, and the Persian archers could not do much damage. The Greek center was thin on purpose, but the sides were thick. When the Persian center pushed through the middle, the Greek sides closed in around it, and the Persians broke. Many ran back to their ships. Herodotus says that 192 Athenians died, and that the Persians lost 6,400.

Historians still argue about the Persian cavalryKAV-ul-reeSoldiers who fight on horseback.. Herodotus does not say it took part. Some think it had been put back on the ships. If so, the Athenians won a battle that they might not have won if the horsemen had been ready. This is another reason Marathon is a story of what could have been.

The Persian fleet did not give up. It sailed around the coast toward Athens, hoping to find it undefended. The Athenian army marched back overnight and stood ready. The Persians saw the army and sailed home.

Why did the Greek charge work? Think about what you learned about the phalanx. Hoplites wore heavy armor and carried long spears. Persian soldiers wore lighter gear and carried shorter spears and bows. In close fighting, the heavier Greeks had a big advantage. The Greeks had also trained to fight shoulder to shoulder. Each man trusted the man beside him. That trust is what held the line.

📚 A Legend and a Legacy

You may have heard that a runner ran from Marathon to Athens to announce the victory, and then died. That is a legendLEJ-undA story handed down from the past that may not be fully true., told in later centuries. Herodotus never mentions it, and he tells us instead about the run of Pheidippides to Sparta. Our modern marathon race remembers the legend, not a documented event.

It is worth asking why such stories last. People like a simple, vivid picture, and a runner collapsing after bringing good news is easy to remember. Careful historians still tell the legend, but they mark it as a legend.

What is certain is that Marathon changed Athens. Its hoplites, the farmers and craftsmen of the middle class, had stood against the great king and won. They gained a deep pride in their city and in themselves. They believed that free men fighting for their own land could face an empire. Darius died in 486 BC, still hoping to punish Athens. His son would come with a much larger army, and Athens would need all that confidence.

Notice, too, who won this battle. These were not nobles fighting for their own glory. They were farmers and craftsmen fighting for their own homes. This is the same middle class that Solon had helped and Cleisthenes had brought into the Assembly. In a way, Athens had trained itself for this day.
