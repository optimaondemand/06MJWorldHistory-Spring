📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What did Rome's victories cost the people who won them?

By about 146 BC, Rome had no equal in the Mediterranean. Today you will see how its wars brought wealth and power, and how they also tore apart life on the farms of Italy.

📚 The Voice of Cato

Cato is said to have ended every speech with the same sentence, whatever the topic: Carthage must be destroyed. Carthage had no fleet, no empire, and no army. Rome destroyed it anyway. Some Romans felt uneasy even then. This lesson asks what happens to a people who win everything, and then must decide what they owe to the people they have beaten and to their own poor.

About fifty years after Zama, a Roman senator named CatoMAR-kus POR-shus KAY-tohA strict senator who kept demanding that Carthage be destroyed. (Marcus Porcius Cato) visited Carthage. He was shocked to see that it had grown rich again. It had no fleet and no empire, but its farms and markets were thriving. Cato went home alarmed. For the rest of his life, he is said to have ended speeches in the Senate with the same line: Carthage must be destroyed.

Was Carthage really a danger? Many historians doubt it. But Romans had not forgotten Hannibal. A rich Carthage seemed like a threat, and fear grew into a plan.

📚 The End of Carthage

In 149 BC, Rome found a reason to go to war. It demanded that Carthage hand over its weapons and then told the Carthaginians to leave their city and move inland. They refused. Carthage held out for three years against a Roman siege. In 146 BC, a Roman general named Scipio Aemilianus broke through the walls. The fight in the streets lasted a week. When it was over, the city was burned, and most of the survivors were sold as slaves. The land became a Roman provincePROV-insA land outside Italy ruled by Rome through an official sent from the city. called Africa. Many years later, people told a story that the Romans spread salt on the fields, but historians doubt this.

Think about what Rome had done. Carthage had given up its empire, its navy, and its army. It was no threat. Yet Rome destroyed it anyway. Some Romans, even then, felt uneasy about this. They wondered whether a city that grew so powerful could keep its old habits of fairness and restraint. Fear and pride had pushed Rome to a cruel act.

📚 Greece Falls Under Rome

At the same time, Rome was fighting in the east. It had fought wars against Macedon, the old kingdom of Alexander's father, Philip. In 168 BC, the Roman general Aemilius Paullus crushed the Macedonian army at Pydna. A few years later, Macedonia was made into a province. Then, in 146 BC, the same year Carthage fell, a league of Greek cities rose against Rome. Rome's army took the rich city of Corinth, burned it, and carried off its art.

From that time, Greece was part of the Roman world. Many Romans admired Greek learning. They hired Greek teachers and copied Greek buildings and statues. But the Greeks themselves now answered to a Roman governor. Think back to the Athens and Sparta you studied last quarter. The freedom of the independent city-states was finished.

📚 Why It Mattered

After these wars, no other power could stand against Rome in the Mediterranean. It now had provinces in Sicily, Spain, Africa, and Macedonia. A Roman governor ruled each province and collected taxes. Some governors were honest, but others grew rich by squeezing the people they ruled. The wealth of the world began to flow into Rome.

📚 The Cost at Home

But the same wars that made Rome powerful hurt many of its citizens. Think about the citizen-soldier. He was a farmer who served for a season and then went home. Now wars lasted for years and took place across the sea. A man might be away for a long time, and his farm went to ruin. Hannibal's army had also burned fields in Italy. Many farmers had to sell their land.

Rich Romans bought up this land. They built huge farms called great estateslat-ih-FUN-dee-uhHuge farms owned by the rich and worked by slaves. (latifundia). These were worked by slaves, who were captured in the wars and cost little. A free farmer could not compete with slave labor. A rich man could buy slaves and let them work the land all year, but a farmer had only his own two hands. So many families lost their farms and drifted to the city of Rome. There they found little work, and they lived in crowded buildings. To feed the poor, the government later began selling cheap grain. This was the grain doleKOO-ruh uh-NOH-nyThe program that sold cheap grain to poor citizens in Rome. (Cura Annonae).

      📚 The Gracchi

      Some Romans saw the danger. In 133 BC, a tribuneTRIB-yoonAn official chosen to protect the common people of Rome. named Tiberius Gracchus proposed a reformrih-FORMA change meant to fix something that is wrong.. Much of the public land had been taken over by the rich. He wanted to take back the extra and give it in small plots to poor citizens. This would give Rome more farmers and more soldiers.

      Many senators were angry, for they were among those who held the land. When another tribune tried to veto the law, Tiberius had him removed from office. That broke a custom, and it made his enemies fear him more. When he ran for a second term as tribune, a mob of senators and their friends clubbed him to death. Hundreds of his followers died too. It was the first time in generations that Romans had killed each other over politics.

      Ten years later, Tiberius's younger brother Gaius took up the cause. He passed a law to sell grain cheaply to the poor and tried to settle citizens in colonies. He too made many enemies. In 121 BC, the Senate declared him an enemy, and he died in the fighting. Thousands of his supporters were killed. The GracchiGRAK-eyeThe brothers Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus, who tried to help poor Romans. had tried to fix a real problem. Their failure showed that the Republic could no longer settle its quarrels by bargaining.
