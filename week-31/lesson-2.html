📚 A City for Sale

Imagine a soldier whose only home is the army. He has no farm to go back to, no trade, and no land. His general pays him, feeds him, and promises him a future when the fighting ends. Whom does he follow when the general and the state disagree? This lesson shows how Rome made that question real, and a young man named Caesar was watching.

The Gracchi were gone, but the troubles they warned about were still there. In North Africa, a king named Jugurthajoo-GER-thuhThe king of Numidia who bribed Roman senators. (Iugurtha) saw how Rome worked, and he decided to use it. He seized his kingdom after a family quarrel, and some Romans were killed in the process. Rome sent officials to deal with him. Jugurtha bribed many of them, including senators. The Roman writer Sallust says Jugurtha called Rome a city for sale, and he said it would perish as soon as it found a buyer.

This made many ordinary Romans furious. Their sons were dying in wars, and nobles were taking bribes. The war in Numidia dragged on for years. Roman generals sent to deal with Jugurtha made deals with him or failed to win. It looked as if the men in charge did not care. People began to ask for someone who would not take bribes.

📚 A New Man Takes Command

That man was MariusGY-us MAIR-ee-usThe Roman general who changed the army and was elected consul seven times. (Gaius Marius). He came from the small town of Arpinum, not from an old noble family. Romans called such a man a new manNOH-vus HOH-mohA Roman whose family had never held high office before. (novus homo). He had won fame as a soldier, and he said openly that the nobles were weak. In 107 BC, the voters made him consul. He took command of the war and brought it to an end, and the people loved him for it.

But Marius faced a problem. Rome needed more soldiers, and the old rule limited the army to men who owned some land. Fewer and fewer citizens did, because so many farms had been lost. So Marius made a change. He let poor men join, even if they had no property. The state gave them weapons and armor, and they served for many years.

📚 The Army of the Poor

It was a smart reformrih-FORMA change meant to fix something that is wrong. in many ways. Rome now had plenty of soldiers, and the poor had steady pay and a way to rise in the world. But there was a hidden danger. A soldier who owned a farm could return to it. A soldier with no land had nothing to go back to. When he finished his service, he would need land, and the Senate often refused to give it. The only person who could help him was his general.

As a result, soldiers began to look to their general for pay, loyalty, and a future. They stopped thinking of themselves as citizens who served Rome, and they thought of themselves as the general's men. A general who won battles and shared the spoils could count on his veteransVET-er-unA soldier who has served for many years and has left the army. to follow him anywhere. This was a great change in the Roman Republic. Think about the difference. A soldier who obeys the Senate fights for Rome. A soldier who obeys his general fights for a man, and that man may someday want something the Republic does not allow. The army had once been the whole people, and now it could be a general's private tool.

      📚 Barbarians at the Gates

      Marius soon proved his worth. Two tribes, the Cimbri and the Teutones, swept down from the north and destroyed several Roman armies. Rome was afraid, for the Gauls had once burned the city. The people elected Marius consul again and again, five years in a row, even though custom said a man should wait ten years before holding the office a second time. He trained a new army and defeated the invaders in 102 and 101 BC.

      Think about what this shows. Rome could survive only by breaking its own rules. Each emergency gave one man more power. That was the way danger had become a habit.

      📚 The Social War

      Another crisis came from within Italy. The Italian allies had fought in Rome's armies for generations, but they were not citizens. They asked for citizenship, and many Romans refused. In 91 BC, the allies rose in revolt. This was called the Social War, from the Latin word for allies. It was bitter and bloody. Rome finally gave citizenship to most of the Italians, and the Republic grew bigger. But Rome had almost lost the heart of Italy.

      📚 Sulla Marches on Rome

      Among Rome's generals in that war was SullaLOO-shus kor-NEEL-yus SUL-uhThe Roman general who marched his army on Rome and made himself dictator. (Lucius Cornelius Sulla), a noble who was an old rival of Marius. In 88 BC, Sulla was elected consul and was given command of a war against King Mithridates in the east. Then a tribuneTRIB-yoonAn official chosen to protect the common people of Rome. tried to take the command away and give it to Marius. Sulla was furious. He led his army to the gates of Rome and entered the city. No Roman general had ever done that.

      Sulla later returned from the east and won a civil war. In 82 BC, he had himself named dictator, and this time there was no six-month limit. He posted lists of names, called proscriptionsproh-SKRIP-shunA public list of people declared enemies of the state, who could be killed and robbed., of men declared enemies of the state. Anyone could kill them and keep a share of their goods. Thousands died. Sulla made the Senate stronger, and then he gave up power in 79 BC and retired. But he had shown something that others noticed. A Roman general with loyal legions could take the city, and the laws could not stop him.

        📚 Weigh It Yourself

        Marius gave poor men a way to earn a decent living, and he also made armies loyal to generals instead of to the state. Was his reform good or bad? Could Rome have solved its problems another way? Write your answer in your notebook with one reason.
