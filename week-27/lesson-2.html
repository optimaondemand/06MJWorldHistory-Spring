📚 The Generals Divide the Empire

Imagine a great library in a foreign city, where thinkers were paid to read, measure, and think. One of them worked out the size of the Earth by comparing the shadow of a stick in two places. This was not a dream. It happened in Alexandria, in a world where Greek kings ruled lands that were not Greek. What happens when learning, power, and different peoples all meet in one place?

After Alexander died in 323 BC, his generals fought for forty years. In the end, three great kingdoms stood. PtolemyTOL-uh-meeAlexander's general who took Egypt and founded a line of Greek kings there. (Ptolemaios) took Egypt, and his family ruled it for almost three hundred years. Seleucussih-LOO-kusAlexander's general who took most of the old Persian lands in Asia. (Seleukos) took most of the old Persian lands, from Syria far to the east. It was a huge kingdom, and hard to hold. The family of another general held Macedonia. Why did the empire break up? Alexander's heir was only a baby, and no one on the field was strong enough to hold everything together. Each general wanted the biggest piece.

We call this age Hellenistichel-uh-NISS-tikGreek-like. It describes the time and the lands where Greek culture spread after Alexander.. The word means "Greek-like." Greek kings ruled, and Greek became the language of government. But do not imagine one blended world. In Egypt, in Syria, and in the far east, most people were not Greek. They kept their own gods, laws, and languages. The Greeks ruled, and they lived in their own cities.

      📚 Greek Cities in Asia

      The new kings founded many cities, often named after themselves. Seleucus built Antioch in Syria and others. Alexander himself had founded several cities named Alexandria. Greek soldiers and settlers moved in and built theaters, marketplaces, and gymnasiums, the places where Greek men exercised and talked.

      As a result, Greek became the common language of trade across many lands. A merchant could travel from Egypt to Bactria and do business in Greek. This is one reason the Greek language lasted so long. Money, ideas, and goods moved along these roads, and some Greeks even grew rich on that trade.

        📚 Alexandria: City of Learning

        The most famous of these cities was Alexandria in Egypt. The Ptolemies built there a great library and a center called the Museum, and they paid scholarsSKOL-erA person who spends life studying and learning. to study. Some of the most gifted people of the age worked there. Books in those days were copied by hand on rolls of papyrus. A library needed a great deal of money and many workers. The kings of Egypt wanted Alexandria to be the center of Greek learning, and they spent money to make it so.

        EuclidYOO-klidThe Greek mathematician who wrote a famous book on geometry. (Eukleides) wrote the Elements, a book on geometry that students used for over two thousand years. Eratosthenesair-uh-TOSS-thuh-neezThe scholar of Alexandria who measured the size of the Earth. (Eratosthenes) led the Library. He noticed that at noon on a certain day, a well in southern Egypt had no shadow, while in Alexandria a stick did. From that difference and the distance between the cities, he worked out how big the Earth is. His answer was remarkably close to the real one.

        Archimedesar-kuh-MEE-deezThe great inventor and mathematician of Syracuse. (Archimedes) lived in Syracuse, in Sicily. He studied levers, circles, and floating objects, and he designed clever machines. A story told long afterward says he found a way to test a golden crown by putting it in water. Whatever the truth of the story, his work was real.

        📚 Two Wonders

        Two famous buildings of the age stood near the sea. The Pharos was a tall lighthouse built near Alexandria around 280 BC. It was one of the tallest buildings in the world, and sailors could see its fire from far away. The Colossus was a giant bronze statue of the sun god on the island of Rhodes. The Rhodians built it after they survived a long siege. It stood about a hundred feet high, and an earthquake knocked it down some fifty years later. Greeks counted both among the wonders of the world. Both buildings show how rich the new kingdoms and cities had become.

        New schools of thought also grew in this age. The Stoics taught that a person should do his duty and stay calm, whatever happens. The Epicureans taught that a quiet life, free of fear, is the best life. These ideas suited a world where ordinary people no longer shaped the great decisions of kings.

        📚 Two Stones and Two Peoples

        In 196 BC, the priests of Egypt wrote a decree to honor the boy king Ptolemy V. It was carved on a stone in three kinds of writing: Egyptian hieroglyphsHY-ruh-glifsThe picture writing of ancient Egypt., an everyday Egyptian script, and Greek. We call it the Rosetta Stone. It shows Greek kings ruling Egypt while Egyptian priests still ran the temples and wrote in their own way. Think about what life was like for an Egyptian under Greek rule. You could pray in your own temple and speak your own language. But the king and his officials spoke Greek, and anyone who wanted a government job had to learn it. French soldiers found the stone in 1799. Later, a scholar used the Greek part to read the hieroglyphs for the first time in centuries.

        The same thing happened far to the east. In India, a king named Asokauh-SHOH-kuhThe Indian king who had his laws carved on rocks and pillars. (Ashoka), the grandson of Chandragupta, had his edictsEE-diktAn official order or announcement from a ruler. carved on rocks and pillars about 250 BC. They urged his people to be kind and honest. A few were written in Greek, for the Greeks who lived in his kingdom. The Greek language was now so common that even an Indian king used it.
