📚 Two Ways of Life Go to War

In 431 BC, Athens and Sparta went to war. The fighting lasted twenty-seven years, so we speak of it as one long war. We call it the Peloponnesian War, after the Peloponnese, the southern part of Greece where Sparta lived.

You already know these two cities well. Athens was a democracy with a great fleet. Sparta was an oligarchy with the best army in Greece. Each had friends. Sparta led a group of cities called the Peloponnesian Leaguepel-uh-puh-NEE-zhun LEEGThe group of cities that were allies of Sparta.. Athens led the cities of its sea empire, which paid tribute and sent ships. So this was more than a quarrel between two cities. It was a contest between two ways of ruling, and almost every Greek city soon had to pick a side.

Both sides also helped their friends inside other cities. Where Athens had the upper hand, the common people gained power. Where Sparta had the upper hand, a few rich men usually ruled. Old quarrels inside cities grew bitter, because each side now had a powerful friend to call on.

📚 The Man Who Wrote It Down

Thucydidesthoo-SID-ih-deezThe Athenian writer who recorded the war between Athens and Sparta. (Thoukydides), whom you met in the last lesson, was an Athenian. He began to write as soon as the war began, because he believed it would be a great one. He did not only tell stories. He asked what caused the war, and he watched closely how people act when they are afraid and angry.

He was also a soldier. Later, Athens sent him into exile after he failed to save a city in the north. That gave him a chance to see both sides of the war. For that reason his book is still read today.

Thucydides named two kinds of causes. The first were the quarrels that set off the fighting. The second, he said, was the truest cause, even though people spoke of it least. It was the growth of Athenian power and the fear that this caused in Sparta. Think about what that means. Sparta did not need to be attacked to feel afraid. It was enough that Athens kept growing stronger.

📚 Three Sparks

Thucydides lists many quarrels. Three of them matter most for us.

The first was at Corcyra, an island on the west coast of Greece. Corcyra had a large fleet. It was a colony of Corinth, but the two were bitter enemies. In 433 BC, Corcyra asked Athens for help against Corinth. Athens did not want Corinth to gain Corcyra's ships, so it made a defensive alliance and sent a small fleet. Corinth was one of Sparta's allies, and it was furious.

The second was at Potidaea. This city lay in the north. It was a colony of Corinth, yet it also paid tribute to Athens. Athens feared it would revolt, and ordered it to weaken its defenses. Potidaea revolted in 432 BC instead, and Corinth helped it. Athens answered with a siegeSEEJAn attack in which an army surrounds a city and cuts it off until it gives up..

The third was at Megara, a small city beside Athens. Athens had shut the people of Megara out of the harbors and markets of its empire. The Megarians called this a cruel hardship, and they carried their complaint to Sparta.

Corinth and its friends urged the Spartans to act. The Spartans voted that Athens had broken the peace between them. Notice that no single event forced the choice. Each city made decisions, and each might have chosen another way. If Athens had stayed out of the quarrel at Corcyra, the war might have been delayed. But the fear that Thucydides described made war seem wiser than waiting.

📚 Pericles Plans to Wait

Sparta struck first. Each summer a Spartan army marched into the land around Athens and burned the farms and orchards. Spartan hoplites were the best in Greece, and Athens could not beat them in an open field.

Pericles had a different strategySTRAT-uh-jeeA big plan for how to win a war.. He told the Athenians to give up their land and trust the sea. Farm families moved inside the Long WallsLONG WAWLZTwo walls that ran from Athens to its harbor and kept the road to the sea safe., two walls that joined the city to its harbor at Piraeus. Because of those walls, Athens could not be starved out while its fleet kept the grain ships coming. Meanwhile the fleet sailed around the Peloponnese and raided Sparta's allies on the coast. Pericles believed that Athens was richer and could outlast Sparta, if it stayed calm and patient and took no big risks.

It was a sound plan, but it asked a great deal. Farmers watched smoke rise from their burning fields, and they were told to do nothing. Many wanted to march out and fight. Pericles held them back, though not everyone loved him for it.

📚 The Plague

In 430 BC, a terrible plaguePLAYGA deadly sickness that spreads quickly from person to person. struck the crowded city. Thucydides caught it himself and lived, so he wrote down what he saw. People burned with fever. Doctors could not help, and many of them died too. Bodies lay unburied.

Something else broke down as well. Thucydides says that people began to lose their fear of the laws and of the gods. Many thought they might be dead tomorrow, so why should they obey? A city is held together by habits of duty and self-control. When those fail, the damage lasts longer than the sickness.

The Athenians blamed Pericles for the war and fined him. Soon they chose him as leader again. But he fell ill and died in 429 BC. It was a heavy loss. Thucydides thought that the leaders who came after him were more alike in ability, and that each wanted to be first. So they began to follow the wishes of the crowd instead of guiding it.

📚 A Peace That Did Not Hold

The war dragged on and neither side could win. Both grew tired. In 421 BC, an Athenian general named NiciasNISH-ee-usA careful, wealthy Athenian general who helped make the peace of 421 BC. (Nikias), a careful and wealthy man, helped to make a peace that was meant to last fifty years. We call it the Peace of Nicias. But the two sides did not trust each other, and some of Sparta's allies would not accept it. The peace was more of a pause than an ending.
