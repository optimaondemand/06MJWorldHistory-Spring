📚 Two Men and a Senate

A small river. A single legion. An order from Rome's leaders to give up his army and come home. In 49 BC, Caesar stood on the bank and had to choose. Whatever he chose, Rome would never be the same. Was the choice his alone, or had Rome's leaders already made it for him?

In 54 BC, Caesar's daughter Julia died. She had been the wife of Pompey, and her marriage had helped hold the two men together. A year later Crassus was killed by the Parthians, and the triumviratetry-UM-vur-itA group of three men who share power. was broken. Only Pompey and Caesar were left.

Pompey now drew close to the Senate. Pompey had his own reasons. In 52 BC, riots in Rome had grown so bad that the Senate made him the only consul, which broke the old rules. He now stood as the Senate's protector. He and Caesar had once been friends, but old friendships were hard to keep in such a time. Many senators feared Caesar and his army in Gaul. They also remembered that the Republic had rules, and Caesar had bent most of them. Caesar needed to come home and run for consul again. But as soon as his command ended, his enemies could take him to court for what he had done as consul. A man in court could be ruined, even exiled.

So Caesar asked for a way out. He wanted to run for consul while still in Gaul, with his army. The Senate said no. In early 49 BC it ordered him to give up his army and come home as a private man. Two tribunesTRIB-yoonA Roman officer elected to protect the rights of ordinary citizens. who tried to speak for Caesar were threatened and fled to his camp.

📚 The Rubicon

The Rubicon was a small river that marked the border between Gaul and Italy. A governor was not allowed to lead an army past that line. To do so was to make war on Rome.

In January of 49 BC, Caesar had only one legion with him. He stood at the river. According to the writer Plutarch, he hesitated for a long time and talked over the choice with his friends. Then he crossed. Another ancient writer says he cried out the die is castAH-lay-uh YAHK-tah ESTA Latin saying that Caesar is reported to have spoken at the Rubicon. (alea iacta est), meaning there was no going back. We cannot be sure he said it. But the words fit the moment.

Here is the key question. Could he have turned back? He could, and he would have faced court and perhaps exile. Could the Senate have let him run for consul? It could, and the war might have been avoided. Neither side wanted to give way, and each thought the other was the aggressor. That is how most wars begin. Think about the choices on both sides. Each could have stepped back and neither did. The Republic did not die because of one river. It died because Roman leaders had spent years refusing to trust one another.

Notice how the two sides tell the story. Caesar, in his own book on the war, says he was defending his honor and the tribunes' rights. Plutarch, who wrote about 150 years later, makes the crossing a dramatic gamble. Each version is shaped by what the writer wanted readers to feel.

      📚 A Quick Victory

      Pompey and most of the Senate fled Rome. They had more soldiers on paper, but those soldiers were far off or not yet trained. Caesar's army marched fast, and towns opened their gates to him. In a few weeks he held all of Italy. He did not rule through terror. He pardoned many captured enemies, and this made him popular. CatoMAR-kus POR-shus KAY-tohA strict, honest senator who sided with Pompey and refused to bow to Caesar., the strictest senator, would not accept any pardon and kept fighting.

      Pompey took his army to Greece. In 48 BC the armies met at Pharsalus. Pompey had far more men, but Caesar's veteransVET-er-unA soldier who has served for many years and has left the army. were steady. They had fought together for years. Pompey's horsemen charged, and they were turned back by a hidden line of Caesar's best foot soldiers. Then Pompey's army broke and ran. Caesar's tactics won the day. Pompey escaped and sailed to Egypt, hoping the young king would help him.

      📚 Alexandria

      The advisers of the boy king Ptolemy XIIITOL-uh-meeThe boy king of Egypt, who ruled beside his sister Cleopatra. did not want to be caught in a Roman quarrel. As Pompey stepped ashore, he was killed. When Caesar arrived days later, they proudly presented him with Pompey's head. Caesar is said to have been horrified, and to have wept.

      Caesar stayed in Alexandria. The king was fighting a war with his sister Cleopatraklee-oh-PAT-ruhThe Greek-speaking queen of Egypt, a descendant of one of Alexander's generals., who was about twenty-one. She had herself smuggled into Caesar's rooms, and the two soon joined forces. Caesar was cut off for months by the king's army, but he won in the end and put Cleopatra on the throne. She later had a son, whom she said was Caesar's. The queen's wealth and ships would matter a great deal later.

      📚 The Last Armies

      Pompey's supporters had not given up. In 46 BC, Caesar beat their army at Thapsus in North Africa. Cato took his own life rather than live under Caesar. In 45 BC, at Munda in Spain, Caesar crushed the last Pompeian army in a hard fight.

      Now every rival was beaten. The civil warSIV-ul WORA war between groups of people who live in the same country. was over. Caesar had held the greatest army in Rome and used it against the Senate, and he had won. The Republic had never faced a man like this. It had rules for kings, for consuls, and for dictators who served a few months. It had no rule for a man who needed none.
