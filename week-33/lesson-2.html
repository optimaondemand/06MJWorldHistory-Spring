📚 A Merciful Master

Caesar came home from his wars in 46 BC to celebrate four great victory parades, called triumphs. The crowds cheered, and he gave every soldier a bonus and every poor citizen a gift of grain and money.

He also showed clemencyKLEM-un-seeMercy shown to an enemy or wrongdoer.. He forgave nearly all the Romans who had fought against him, and some of them were given high office. The Senate even voted to build a temple to his mercy. Among those he forgave was BrutusMAR-kus JOO-nee-us BROO-tusThe senator, once Caesar's friend, who became the chief conspirator., who had fought for Pompey. Caesar treated Brutus almost like a son.

Caesar then made a long list of changes. Many were good, and some looked far ahead. He reformed the calendar with the help of an astronomer from Alexandria, so that the year had 365 days and every fourth year had one extra. This Julian calendar is the ancestor of the one we use today, and the month of July is named for him. He gave land to his veterans. He let leading men from Gaul and other provinces into the Senate and gave citizenship to more people. He also made the Senate much larger, which many old senators resented.

📚 Too Much Honor

Yet Caesar kept gathering power. The Senate gave him statues, a golden chair, and the right to wear a special purple robe. In early 44 BC, he was named dictatorDIK-tay-turA ruler with total power. In early Rome, a dictator held it for only six months in a crisis. for life. Rome's old dictators had served for only six months. A dictator for life had no such limit. Some of the honors were flattery, and some were fear. Either way, each new one made Caesar look less like a fellow citizen and more like a ruler. Old Romans noticed, and they did not like it.

Then came a strange scene. At the festival of the Wolf Festivalloo-per-KAY-lee-uhAn old Roman festival held each February, with a race through the city streets. (Lupercalia), Mark AntonyMAR-kus an-TOH-nee-usCaesar's loyal friend and general, who was consul in 44 BC. pushed a royal crown toward Caesar in front of the crowd. Caesar pushed it away. Antony offered it again, and again Caesar refused it. Many people cheered each refusal. Was it a test of the crowd? Was it a show planned in advance? Ancient writers could not agree, and neither can we.

Whatever it meant, Romans remembered the old story of the kings and how the city had thrown them out. To many senators, a ruler who could not be removed was a king in all but name.

📚 The Plot

Around sixty men joined a plot to assassinateuh-SASS-in-aytTo kill an important person, often for political reasons. him. Brutus and CassiusGY-us KASH-us lon-JY-nusA soldier and senator who helped plan Caesar's killing. were the leaders. Cassius was a hard soldier who hated Caesar. Brutus was a thoughtful man who admired the old Republic. His family told a story that one of his ancestors had driven out Rome's last king. Friends left notes on his chair, urging him to live up to that name.

Why would a man kill his own friend? Brutus believed that a Roman's first duty was to the city and its laws, not to any person. He had been spared by Caesar, and he thought that very fact proved the problem. If one man could grant or take away life at will, then Romans were no longer free citizens. Later, Brutus put out a coin with two daggers and the words "Ides of March" on it, to show what he had done. Brutus saw the deed as a duty. Caesar's friends saw it as murder. The question of who was right has divided people ever since.

📚 The Ides of March

The conspirators chose the IdesEYEDZThe middle day of a Roman month. In March it was the fifteenth. of March (Idus Martiae), which was March 15 in 44 BC. The Senate was meeting in a hall near the theater of Pompey. Antony was kept outside by a trick. The conspirators crowded around Caesar as if to make a request, and then drew their daggers. He was stabbed many times and fell at the foot of a statue of his old rival, Pompey. Plutarch says the great man lay dead at the base of that statue.

Then came a shock. The senators ran away in fear. The streets were nearly empty, and Brutus tried to speak to the people. They did not cheer. They did not know what to think.

📚 What They Did Not Plan

The assassins had thought only about the knife, and almost nothing about the morning after. They had not planned to remove Antony, who held a great deal of power. They had no army, no plan for the city, and no plan to win over Caesar's soldiers. They hoped the Republic would simply return, as if one blow could undo years of change.

It did not. At Caesar's funeral, Antony, who was a gifted speaker, read out his will, which left money to every citizen. He also showed the crowd Caesar's bloody robe. The crowd grew furious. They tore up benches from nearby buildings and burned the body in the Forum, and Brutus and Cassius had to flee the city. Here is the sad twist. The men who wanted to save the Republic had made certain it would be fought over by armies once again.

💪 KEEP THIS IN MIND

The knives had done their work in a few minutes, but they had not saved the Republic. Caesar's soldiers, his friend Antony, and a crowd that loved him were all still there. The assassins had killed a man. They had not killed what he stood for.
