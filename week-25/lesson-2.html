📚 Sparta Rules, and Greeks Resent It

Sparta said it fought to free the Greeks. Then it won, and it put its own men in charge of the very cities it had promised to free. In the end, the Great King of Persia was the one who told Greeks where their borders lay. This lesson is about what happens to a promise of freedom when the winner finds that power is easier than keeping its word.

In 404 BC, Sparta stood at the top. It had beaten Athens and taken the empire that Athens had ruled. Many Greek cities hoped for freedom. They had been told that Sparta fought to set Greece free.

What they got instead was a new master. Sparta put friendly oligarchs in power in many cities, and it placed a garrisonGAIR-ih-sunA group of soldiers stationed in a town to control it. in some of them. Spartan officers gave orders where Athenian ones had given them before. Spartiates had trained all their lives for war, and they had not been trained to rule other people gently. A city that had trained its men for war now had to rule dozens of other cities, and that is a different job from fighting. Within a few years, the Greek cities hated Sparta as they had once hated Athens.

Some Greeks went to war against Sparta, including Thebes, Corinth, Argos, and Athens. During that war, Persian money turned up again, this time on the other side. It helped Athens begin to rebuild its fleet.

📚 The King's Peace

In 387 BC, the war ended in a strange way. The Persian king sent word of the terms he wanted, and the Greek cities agreed. The Greek cities of Ionia, on the coast of Asia Minor, would belong to Persia. All other Greek cities would be free and rule themselves. Sparta was chosen to make sure everyone obeyed. Greek leaders had learned that Persian money could win Greek wars, so they kept going to the Persian king for help. In the end, it was the king who decided how the war would end.

Think about what this means. A few generations earlier, Greeks had fought at Marathon and Salamis so that Persia would not rule them. Now the Great King could tell Greeks where their borders lay. They had won the war against Persia, but they had lost the peace to their own quarrels. This is a lesson about what disunity can cost.

Sparta used the peace to break up leagues and to keep other cities small. It even seized the high fort of Thebes by trick in 382 BC. The Thebans rose against the garrison a few years later, and they threw it out.

    📚 Epaminondas and the Sacred Band

    Thebes was the main city of Boeotia, north of Athens. Its leader in these years was Epaminondaseh-pam-ih-NON-dasThe Theban general who defeated Sparta at Leuctra. (Epameinondas), a general. He was a man of great skill and plain living. He trained the Theban army hard and added a picked unit of three hundred men called the Sacred Band.

    In 371 BC, the Spartan army met the Thebans at Leuctra, in Boeotia. The Spartan king led them. Normally, the best soldiers stood at the right end of the line. So the Spartans put their finest men there, about twelve rows deep. Epaminondas did the opposite. He packed his best men on his own left, fifty rows deep, and aimed them straight at the Spartan king and his Spartiates.

    The Spartans could not hold. Their king was killed, and so were hundreds of Spartiates. Sparta had very few full citizens by this time, and it could not replace them. The most feared army in Greece had been beaten in open battle. The result shocked all of Greece. Think how the Spartans must have felt when their line did not hold. They had always won such battles. Their whole way of life rested on the idea that Spartan soldiers did not lose.

    Historians still marvel at the plan. It was a case where a brilliant idea changed history. Had Epaminondas used the usual formation, Leuctra might have turned out the other way.

    📚 Messenia Goes Free

    Epaminondas did not stop. He led an army into the Peloponnese itself, and he marched to the land of Messenia, west of Sparta. For centuries the Messenians had been helots, working Sparta's fields. Epaminondas set them free and helped them build a walled city of their own. Many Messenians had been born into servitude and had never known anything else. Now they were citizens of a city that belonged to them.

    This was a heavy blow. Remember how Sparta lived: the Spartiates were soldiers, and the helots grew their food. Without Messenia, Sparta lost much of its land and its workers. Its power never came back.

    📚 No City Can Lead

    Thebes now thought it should lead Greece. But other cities would not accept it, just as they had refused to accept Sparta and Athens before. In 362 BC, the Thebans fought a last great battle at Mantinea. They won, but Epaminondas was killed there, and Thebes' brief moment passed with him.

    XenophonZEN-uh-fonAn Athenian soldier and writer who admired Sparta and wrote about this time. (Xenophon) was an Athenian who admired Sparta and who had known Socrates. He wrote the history of this time, called the Hellenica. At the end of the book, he says that after Mantinea there was even more confusion and disorder in Greece than before. Notice how sad the tone is. No city had been able to lead, and no city would follow.

    So the Greeks were tired, divided, and poor in men. They had no leader. In the north, a kingdom that most Greeks thought of as half-foreign was getting stronger. That is where we go next.

    📚 Weigh It Yourself

    Sparta promised freedom and then gave orders. What does a leader owe to the promises that helped win the war? Think about what Sparta lost by breaking its word. Write your answer in your notebook with one reason.

    💪 KEEP THIS IN MIND

    After the war, no Greek city could keep the lead for long. Sparta lost it at Leuctra, and Thebes lost it at Mantinea. The Greeks had shown they could beat each other, but not that they could live together in peace. That left room for someone from outside the old system. Ask a classmate to name a promise that is hard to keep, and talk about why people keep it anyway.
