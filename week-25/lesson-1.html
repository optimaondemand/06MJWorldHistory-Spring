📚 The Thirty and the Return of Democracy

After the war, Lysanderly-SAN-derThe Spartan admiral who won the war at sea. (Lysandros) and Sparta helped thirty rich Athenians take power in 404 BC. They are called the Thirty Tyrants. The harshest of them was CritiasKRIT-ee-usThe harshest leader of the Thirty, and once a companion of Socrates. (Kritias). He had once been a companion of Socrates, and so had Alcibiadesal-sih-BY-uh-deezA brilliant, daring Athenian who urged the attack on Sicily and later changed sides. (Alkibiades). Remember that, because it will matter later.

The Thirty put many Athenians to death and took their property. Hundreds were killed, and many others fled. A democrat named Thrasybulusthras-ih-BYOO-lusThe Athenian exile who led the democrats in winning back the city. (Thrasyboulos) gathered the exiles in Thebes. With a small band, he seized a fort in the hills and then the harbor at Piraeus. Critias was killed in the fighting. The Thirty fell, and in 403 BC the democracy came back.

The Athenians then did something wise. They passed an amnestyAM-nuh-steeA promise not to punish people for wrongs they did in the past.. Citizens swore not to punish each other for what had been done during the war and the tyranny. It was meant to heal the city. But you will see it also had a side effect.

📚 The Man Who Asked Questions

SocratesSOK-ruh-teezThe Athenian thinker who questioned people about justice and goodness. (Sokrates) was the son of a stonemason. He fought as a hoplite and had a reputation for courage. He is remembered as a philosopherfuh-LOSS-uh-ferA person who searches for wisdom by asking deep questions about life., a seeker of wisdom. He did not write books. We know him mostly from his students, especially Plato.

Socrates walked about the marketplace of Athens and asked people questions. What is justice? What is courage? What makes a good life? Again and again he showed that people who sounded certain could not really explain their own words. In Plato's account, a friend once asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, and the oracle said no. Socrates was puzzled. He questioned famous men to find someone wiser. He decided he was wiser only in this way: he knew that he did not know.

You can imagine how the men he questioned felt. Many were embarrassed, and some were angry. Socrates also disagreed with the sophists you met in Lesson 23.2. Some of them taught that justice was only an agreement between people, or that the strong should rule. Socrates believed that there is a real justice and goodness a person can look for, and that a good soul matters more than money or fame. He would have disagreed with the Athenians at Melos.

📚 The Trial of 399 BC

In 399 BC, three men brought Socrates to court. Meletusmeh-LEE-tusThe man who brought the formal charges against Socrates. (Meletos) was the one who made the formal charge. They said that Socrates did not honor the gods of the city, that he brought in new spiritual things, and that he led young men astray.

Think about who these accusers were. Anytus, one of them, had fought against the Thirty. They were not tyrants. They were democrats who loved their city. It was in some ways a trial of philosophy, and in some ways a trial of how far a democracy can allow questioning.

The jury was made up of hundreds of Athenian citizens chosen by lot. Each side spoke in a single day. Plato was there, and he wrote down what Socrates said in a work called the Apology. The word does not mean saying sorry here. It means a defense. Socrates did not beg. He told the jury that he had done the city a service, and that he would not stop asking questions even if they ordered him to.

The jury found him guilty by a fairly close vote. When it was time to name a punishment, Socrates joked that he should be fed for free at the city's expense, like an Olympic champion. The jury was not amused. It voted for death. A month passed because of a holy festival. Friends offered to help him escape, but Socrates refused. He drank the poison hemlockHEM-lokA poisonous plant, used in Athens to carry out a death sentence. and died calmly.

📚 Why Did They Do It?

No one reason explains the verdict. Here are some that historians suggest.

First, the city was frightened and hurt. Two of its worst enemies, Critias and Alcibiades, had been his companions. Many Athenians linked him to them. In fairness, Socrates had also refused an order from the Thirty to help arrest an innocent man, which put his own life at risk.

Second, Socrates openly doubted some democratic customs, such as choosing officials by lot. Third, the comic poet Aristophanes had made him look foolish and dangerous years before. And fourth, the amnesty meant no one could be tried for acts during the tyranny. Some historians think the charge of religion was a way to get around that rule.

Remember also that the vote was close. A few changed votes would have saved him. History often turns on small numbers like these.

📚 Plato and Aristotle

Socrates left a great mark on his students. PlatoPLAY-tohA student of Socrates who wrote down his conversations and founded a school. (Platon) wrote books in which Socrates talks with others. Around 387 BC, he started a school near Athens called the Academy. It lasted for centuries.

AristotleAIR-ih-STOT-ulA student of Plato who studied almost every subject, and later taught Alexander. (Aristoteles) joined Plato's school as a teenager and stayed for about twenty years. He studied nearly everything. He taught that human beings are made to live in a polis, because it is the only place where a person can fully become good. He also studied many constitutions. He sorted them by who rules: one, a few, or many. Each could be good if the rulers cared for the whole people, and bad if they cared only for themselves. Years later, Aristotle would teach a young prince named Alexander. You will meet him soon.

💪 KEEP THIS IN MIND

Athens put Socrates to death for several reasons at once, and the people who judged him were not simple villains. The trial asks a question that every free people must face: how much room should a city give to those who question its ways?
