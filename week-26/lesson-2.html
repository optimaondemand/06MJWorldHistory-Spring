📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How could a young king and a small army overthrow the largest empire in the world?

Alexander was twenty when he became king, and he was thirty-two when he died. In between, he won every great battle he fought. Today you will follow his first four years in Asia.

📚 A King at Twenty

A king at twenty, with a spear in his hand and the world in front of him. Alexander had lost his father, and half of Greece was waiting for him to fail. Instead he crossed the sea with an army smaller than the one Persia could raise in a single province, and within three years he had ended the empire of Cyrus. Bravery, ambition, and cruelty marched in the same army. This lesson asks you to look at all three.

When PhilipFIL-ipThe king of Macedonia who made his kingdom the strongest power in Greece. (Philippos) died in 336 BC, many people thought his kingdom would fall apart. His son Alexanderal-ig-ZAN-derThe son of Philip, who became king at age twenty and conquered Persia. (Alexandros) moved fast. He was twenty years old, but he was not new to war. At Chaeronea, he had led the cavalry. He had grown up in the royal court with his mother, Olympias, and he had been taught by the philosopher Aristotle. He loved the Iliad, the old story of Achilles, and he wanted to be a hero like him.

Greek cities rose against the new king. In 335 BC, Thebes revolted. Alexander marched south with great speed and destroyed the city. Many of its people were killed or sold as slaves. The rest of Greece took the lesson, and the revolts stopped. It was a ruthless act. You will see that Alexander could be kind and cruel in the same year.

📚 Across the Hellespont

In 334 BC, Alexander led about forty thousand men across the Hellespont into Asia. He is said to have thrown his spear into the ground on the far shore, to show that he took the land as a prize of war. Then he visited the ruins of Troy and honored the grave of Achilles.

Think about what he was risking. His army was small beside the Persian Empire, which stretched from Asia Minor to India. If he lost, he would have no easy way home. But the Persian king had a huge land to guard, and Alexander could choose where to strike.

At the Granicus River, the Persian governors waited for him on the far bank. Alexander charged straight across and led the attack himself. In the fight, he was nearly killed, and a friend saved him. The Persians broke and ran. Soon the Greek cities of Ionia were freed from Persian rule, and Sardis opened its gates.

    Next, the Persian king Darius IIIduh-RY-usThe last great king of Persia, who lost his empire to Alexander. (Dareios) came out to meet him with a huge army. In 333 BC, the two armies met at Issus, on a narrow plain between the mountains and the sea. In such a tight space, the Persians could not use their big numbers. Alexander again led a charge on his Companion cavalryheh-TY-royThe riders, from noble families, who fought beside the Macedonian king. (hetairoi), aimed straight at Darius. Darius turned and fled. His mother, wife, and daughters were taken prisoner, and Alexander treated them with respect.

    Notice how much depended on a few choices. Had Darius waited on open ground, his army might have done better. Historians still ask why he fought in that narrow place.

    📚 Tyre and Egypt

    Alexander did not chase Darius. He went south along the coast to take the great port cities, so that the Persian fleet would have no place to land. Persia's ships were crewed by men from those coastal cities. Without the ports, the fleet had nowhere to go. Tyre was an island city about half a mile from the shore, with high walls. Alexander built a causewayKAWZ-wayA raised road built across water or marshy ground. of rubble out to the island. It took seven months, and the Tyrians fought hard. When the city fell in 332 BC, many were killed or sold as slaves.

    Egypt welcomed him. The Egyptians had not liked Persian rule. Egypt was also one of the richest lands in the world, and its grain fed many people. Taking it gave Alexander food, money, and safety for his rear. At the old capital, Memphis, he made sacrifices to the Egyptian gods. On the coast, he marked out a new city and named it Alexandria. Then he crossed the desert to visit the oracleOR-uh-kulA holy place where a god was thought to answer questions, or the priest who spoke for the god. of the god Ammon. The priests greeted him as the god's son. Alexander kept private what the god told him.

    🎯 TRY IT: After Issus
    You are Alexander. You have beaten Darius at Issus, and he has fled. The Persian fleet is still out there. What do you do next?
    What happened: Alexander let Darius go and went after the coast. Tyre fell after a seven-month causeway project, and Egypt welcomed him with food, money, and a safe rear.
    📚 Gaugamela

    Darius had raised a new army and chose his ground this time. He picked a wide, flat plain in what is now Iraq, not far from Arbela. It had plenty of room for horses and for chariots with blades on their wheels. In 331 BC, Alexander crossed the Tigris and approached. It was the biggest test he ever faced. The Persian army was far larger than his, and Darius had picked the ground. Alexander's men were few, but they had been trained to work together.

    One story has the general Parmenion urging a night raid. In that story, Alexander refuses to steal a win. The next day, he led his horsemen in a wedge toward the spot where Darius stood. A gap opened in the Persian line, and Alexander drove through it. Darius fled again, and the Persian army fell apart. Babylon opened its gates to the winner.

    The Persian Empire that Cyrus had built was over in practice, though Darius still lived. In just three years, a young man with a small army had overthrown it. How did he do it? He was brave and quick, and his men trusted him. His army was well trained. But he was also lucky. A blow that landed a little differently at the Granicus, or a Persian king who waited at Issus, might have changed everything.
