📚 A Kingdom in Danger

Philip of Macedon was twenty-three when his brother was killed in battle. His kingdom was surrounded by enemies, and his army was a crowd of farmers. Twenty-one years later, he had beaten the combined armies of Athens and Thebes. As you read, try to answer two questions. How did he do it? And was what he did good for the Greeks?

North of the Greek cities lay Macedonia. It was a land of farms, hills, and horses. It was ruled by a king, not by a city assembly. The Macedonian nobles hunted, rode, and drank with their king, and they could be fierce toward him if they did not like him. Many Greeks thought the Macedonians were half-foreign, though their kings said their family came from Greece and even took part in the Olympic Games.

The kingdom was often weak. Enemies pressed from every side. In 359 BC, the Macedonian king was killed in a battle with tribes from the north. His younger brother, PhilipFIL-ipThe king of Macedonia who made his kingdom the strongest power in Greece. (Philippos), took charge. Philip was about twenty-three, and the future of Macedonia looked grim. Think how hard his task was. He had little money, an army that was not ready, and enemies who expected the kingdom to fall. Many young rulers would have failed.

📚 A Teenager in Thebes

When he was a boy, Philip had been sent to Thebes as a hostageHOSS-tijA person held by another ruler to make sure a promise is kept.. A hostage was a guarantee that his kingdom would keep its word. He lived there for some years, at the time when Epaminondaseh-pam-ih-NON-dasThe Theban general who defeated Sparta at Leuctra. (Epameinondas) was making Thebes great. Philip saw how a trained army could beat a bigger one. We cannot be sure what he learned, but he put it to use.

📚 The Macedonian Machine

Philip rebuilt the army. Macedonian soldiers had been farmers called out when needed. Philip turned them into full-time soldiers who trained and got paid. He gave the foot soldiers a new weapon, the long pikesuh-RISS-uhA spear more than twelve feet long, used by the Macedonian phalanx. (sarissa). It was more than twelve feet long, much longer than a Greek spear. Rows of pikes made a wall that enemies could not easily reach. Next to them rode the Companion cavalryheh-TY-royThe riders, from noble families, who fought beside the Macedonian king. (hetairoi), the sons of nobles. The pike line held the enemy, and the horsemen struck at the weak spot.

Think of the pike line as a moving wall. A hoplite with a shorter spear could not get close to the men behind it. The pikemen did not have to win the fight alone. They only had to hold the enemy in place. Then the cavalry rode around and hit the enemy from the side.

Such an army cost a lot of money. Philip found it. He captured a city that stood near gold mines. They gave him a great deal of gold for coins and wages. Money and power went together here.

Philip used diplomacy as well. He married into other royal families, made treaties, and, it was said, paid bribes. A story, which may or may not be true, says he claimed that any city could be taken if a donkey loaded with gold could get through its gates. Many Greeks thought winning in this way was dishonorable. Philip cared more about winning.

📚 Demosthenes Sounds the Alarm

In Athens, Demosthenesdeh-MOSS-thuh-neezAn Athenian orator who warned his city about Philip. (Demosthenes) was an oratorOR-uh-terA person who is skilled at giving speeches.. He saw what Philip was doing and tried to warn the Athenians. Around 351 BC, he gave a speech we call the First Philippic. He said Athens must act now, before Philip became too strong. He urged the citizens to serve in the army themselves instead of hiring mercenariesMUR-suh-ner-eeA soldier who fights for pay instead of for his own country.. He complained that the Athenians preferred festivals to war.

Demosthenes was not trying to frighten people for no reason. He believed that freedom must be defended by citizens who are ready to give up comfort, time, and money. Most people did not want to hear that.

Some Athenians listened, and others did not. Philip grew stronger year by year. He moved into northern Greece, then into central Greece, and many cities found it easier to deal with him than to fight.

📚 Chaeronea

In 338 BC, Athens and Thebes at last joined forces against Philip. They met his army at Chaeronea, in Boeotia. Philip led one wing of his army, and his son Alexanderal-ig-ZAN-derThe son of Philip, who became king at age twenty and conquered Persia. (Alexandros), who was eighteen, led the cavalry on the other. The Macedonians won. The Sacred Band of Thebes was killed almost to the last man. After the battle, Philip treated Athens gently, but he put a garrison in Thebes.

The next year, Philip called the Greek states together at Corinth. They agreed to keep the peace among themselves and to follow Philip in a war against Persia. Only Sparta refused. The independent city-states of Greece were still there, but they now followed a king. Athens and Thebes still ran their own affairs at home. But they could no longer decide war and peace for themselves.

        📚 A Sudden End

        Philip began to plan the war against Persia. In 336 BC, at a royal wedding in Macedonia, one of his own bodyguards killed him. We do not know all the reasons. Philip was about forty-six. Alexander, who was twenty, became king.

        What if Philip had lived? He might have led the war against Persia himself, and the world might have been quite different. History is full of moments like this, when one life, cut short, changes all that follows.

        Some Greeks saw Philip as a tyrant who ended their freedom. Others saw him as the one man who could end the endless wars between cities. You will read Demosthenes this week and decide for yourself.
