📚 The Great King Returns

Leonidas chose his men with care. He took only Spartans who already had sons, so that no family line would end. Then he marched north to a narrow place in the mountains, knowing the odds. He did not go alone. Several thousand men from other cities went with him, and some of them stayed to the end. What would make a man choose that?

When Darius died in 486 BC, his son XerxesZURK-seezThe king of Persia from 486 to 465 BC, who invaded Greece in 480 BC. (Xerxes) became king of Persia. Xerxes first had to put down a revolt in Egypt. Then he turned to Greece. He wanted to finish what his father had begun, and he planned a huge invasion. According to Herodotus, his army had more than a million men. Historians do not believe this. The real number was much smaller, though still enormous for that time.

The Persians made some astonishing preparations. A canal was dug across the neck of the peninsula at Mount Athos, so that ships could avoid the rough sea where an earlier Persian fleet had been wrecked. Two bridges of boats were built across the HellespontSTRATEA narrow stretch of water connecting two larger seas.. Armies could now cross from Asia into Europe on foot. Everything about the plan said that Persia meant to win.

To get a sense of the size of the effort, think about the problem Xerxes faced. A huge army needs food and water every day. It needs roads and bridges. It needs ships to carry supplies along the coast. Persia spent years gathering all of this. The Greeks, by contrast, had no single ruler and no single plan. Many of them did not know whether they would fight or give in.

📚 Greeks Unite, for Now

Most Greek cities could not imagine joining against Persia. They hated each other, and some gave earth and water to the Persian king, which was a sign of surrender. But about thirty Greek states met near Corinth and swore to fight together in an allianceuh-LY-unssAn agreement between states to help each other, especially in war.. Sparta, the strongest land power, was given command of the whole force. Athens had the biggest fleet.

Choosing Sparta as the leader made sense, because Sparta had the best soldiers in Greece. But it also showed how much Athens, with its great fleet, had to give up for the sake of unity. Pride had to be set aside. This is one more sign of how great the danger felt.

This unity was rare and fragile. The Greeks quarreled about where to make a stand. They chose to hold a narrow place on the road south, and to meet the Persian fleet at sea nearby at the same time.

📚 The Pass of the Hot Gates

They chose a place called Thermopylaether-MOP-ih-leeThe narrow pass on the road to southern Greece, named for its hot springs. (Thermopylai), the Hot Gates, named for its warm springs. Here the mountains came down almost to the sea, and the road through was only wide enough for a few men. A small force could hold off a huge one. King Leonidaslee-ON-ih-dusThe king of Sparta who led the Greeks at Thermopylae. (Leonidas) of Sparta led the Greeks there with three hundred Spartiates and several thousand allies from other cities. He took Spartans who already had sons, so that their families would survive.

Here you can see the value of the phalanx. In a wide field, a huge army can surround a small one. In a narrow passPASSA narrow path through mountains., only a few men can fight at a time. The Greeks' heavy armor and long spears were at their best there. The Persian soldiers, who were more lightly armed, could not break through. The place did as much work as the men.

Herodotus tells how the Persians waited, and then attacked. Greek hoplites held the narrow pass in a tight phalanxFAY-langksA tight block of soldiers who fought shoulder to shoulder in rows.. Wave after wave of Persians fell. Xerxes was said to have leaped from his throne in fear. The Greeks held for two days.

👤 FIGURE SPOTLIGHT · Leonidas

Leonidas was one of the two kings of Sparta. He led the Greek force at Thermopylae in 480 BC, when he was an older man. Herodotus tells us that the Spartans were celebrating a religious festival, which kept most of the army at home, so Leonidas went north with his personal guard of three hundred. Herodotus also says that an oracle had told the Spartans that either Sparta would fall or a Spartan king would die. Whether or not the oracle was real, Leonidas seems to have understood that the stand was likely to cost him his life. He did not retreat. The Spartans honored him greatly afterward. His bones were later brought home to Sparta and given a funeral with games each year. He remains one of the best-known figures in the ancient world.

📚 The Secret Path

A local man named Ephialtesef-ee-AL-teezA Greek who betrayed the secret mountain path to the Persians. He is not the Athenian reformer of the same name you will meet later. (Ephialtes) told the Persians about a goat path over the mountains. He hoped for a reward. Xerxes sent a force along the path at night. When Leonidas heard that the Persians were behind him, he sent most of the allies away. The Spartans stayed, with seven hundred men from the city of Thespiae and some others. They fought until almost everyone was dead.

Think about what Leonidas faced. He could have run, and he could have saved his men. He chose to stay and to hold the pass as long as he could, so that the others could get away. Courage like this was one of the virtues the Greeks admired most. It is also the kind of virtue that history remembers, even when the battle is lost.

The pass had fallen, but at a cost. At the same time, the Greek fleet fought the Persian ships at Artemisium, off the northern tip of Euboea. That battle was not decided, but when the fleet heard of Thermopylae, it withdrew south. The road to Athens now lay open.

Keep the name Ephialtes in mind. Another man with the same name will matter later, but he was a different person.

📚 Why It Matters

Was Thermopylae a victory or a defeat? The Greeks lost the pass, and Xerxes marched on. But the stand showed that Greeks would fight and die rather than submit. It gave time to evacuate Athens and prepare the fleet. And it became a story that later Greeks told to inspire courage. A battle can be lost and still change history. The next lesson shows where the Greeks made their real stand.
