📚 Athens Burns

After Thermopylae, the Persian army marched into Attica. The Athenians had already left. Most of the people were taken to nearby islands and safe cities, and the Persians found an empty town. They burned the Acropolis and its temples. It was a heavy blow. A Greek city had been burned by an enemy, and Persia's king could now claim he had punished Athens.

But Athens was not its buildings. Its people and its fleet were still alive. Some Athenians had understood years before that the sea would be their best defense.

Imagine leaving your home, knowing that it may be burned. The Athenians packed what they could carry and sailed away. Old people, women, and children went to safe places. Men boarded the ships. It was a bold decision. A city that gives up its land to save its people is trusting in something besides stone walls.

📚 A Fleet Built on Silver

The man who saw this was Themistoclesthuh-MIS-tuh-kleezThe Athenian leader who built the fleet and planned the victory at Salamis. (Themistokles). A few years earlier, workers at the Laurion mines in Attica had struck a rich vein of silver. Many Athenians wanted to divide the money among all citizens. Themistocles persuaded them to spend it on ships instead. According to Herodotus, they built about two hundred of these new warships. The ship was the triremeTRY-reemA fast warship with three rows of rowers on each side., a fast vessel with three rows of rowers on each side.

This choice had a lasting effect. A trireme needed about two hundred men to row it. The rowers were the poorest citizens, the thetes, who owned no land. A navy gave them a new role in defending the city. In time, that would help to give them a bigger voice in governing it.

Look at how this fits with what you learned about Solon and Cleisthenes. Athens had been slowly giving more citizens a voice. Now it was giving them a ship to fight from. Many Athenians would soon argue that the people who rowed the triremes had earned a share in running the city. The navy and the democracy would grow together.

⚓ TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT · The Trireme

The trireme was the main warship of the Greek world. It was long, narrow, and light, built to move fast. About one hundred seventy rowers sat in three rows on each side, with the upper rows reaching over the lower ones. At the front, a bronze ram stuck out below the water line. Instead of boarding an enemy ship, a trireme tried to smash into its side and sink it. This required skill and training. The crew had to row in perfect rhythm and turn the ship quickly. A trireme could not carry food for many days and had to land every night. A well-trained fleet of triremes was a deadly weapon in narrow waters, but a clumsy one could be trapped there.

📚 The Trick at Salamis

The Greek fleet gathered near the island of Salamis, just off the coast of Athens. The Greek commanders argued about whether to stay or to sail away to the south. Themistocles wanted to fight in the narrow straitSTRATEA narrow stretch of water connecting two larger seas. where the huge Persian fleet could not use its numbers. To force the matter, Herodotus says he sent a secret message to Xerxes. The message said the Greeks were about to run away and that the king should block the exits.

Xerxes believed it. During the night, Persian ships moved into the strait. In the morning, in September of 480 BC, the Greeks attacked. The Persian ships jammed together in the narrow space, and many crashed into each other. Greek triremes rammed them one by one. Xerxes watched from a seat on the shore. By the end of the day, the Persian fleet was ruined.

Xerxes went home soon after, though he left a strong army behind.

The trick worked because Xerxes wanted to believe it. He thought the Greeks were afraid, and he thought a fast victory was waiting. Think about how an enemy's hopes can be used against him. Themistocles knew the strait would help the Greeks, and he knew the Persian king would fall for a lie. This is why Salamis is remembered as a victory of clever planning as much as of force.

📚 Plataea and Mycale

In 479 BC, the Persian general Mardoniusmar-DOH-nee-usThe Persian general left behind to finish the war in 479 BC. (Mardonios) remained in Greece with the army. The Greeks gathered the largest army they had ever raised. A Spartan commander, Pausaniaspaw-SAY-nee-usThe Spartan leader of the Greek army at Plataea. (Pausanias), led it. The two armies met near the city of Plataea, north of Athens. After days of waiting and skirmishing, the Greek hoplites broke the Persian line. Mardonius was killed, and the Persian army fell apart.

By tradition the same day saw another victory. The Greek fleet beat a Persian force at Mycale, on the coast of Ionia. Together, these battles ended the invasion.

At Plataea, the Greeks won as they had at Marathon. The hoplites held together, and their heavy armor and long spears did the work. Notice that the victory belonged to many cities. Spartans, Athenians, and soldiers from many other cities stood together. That is rare in this story, and it is why it deserves to be remembered.

📚 Unity Was the Exception

Look back at how the Greeks won. They were united by fear, and they had a few good leaders. But many Greek cities sided with Persia. Others stayed out of the fight. Even among the loyal cities, quarrels never stopped. Greek unity was the exception, not the rule.

After the war, Athens, with the largest fleet, was far stronger and prouder than it had been. Sparta, the great land power, was suspicious. The Greeks had won, but the same forces that nearly lost the war would return. The next lesson looks at how Athens used its new power.
