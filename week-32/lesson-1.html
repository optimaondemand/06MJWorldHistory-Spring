📚 The Slave Who Shook Italy

After Sulla, Rome was quiet for a while, but the old troubles remained. One of them was slavery. By the 70s BC, Italy held hundreds of thousands of slaves, many of them captured in Rome's wars. Some were trained to fight in shows for crowds, and they were called gladiatorsGLAD-ee-ay-terA trained fighter, often a slave, who fought other men for public entertainment..

In 73 BC, about seventy of them broke out of a gladiator school in the city of Capua. One was a Thracian named SpartacusSPAR-tuh-kusThe gladiator from Thrace who led a great slave revolt.. He had once been a soldier, and he was a gifted leader. The runaways made camp on the slopes of Mount Vesuvius. Slaves from farms and estates flocked to them, until the army grew to tens of thousands.

Rome was caught by surprise. It sent small forces, and Spartacus beat them. For two years, his army roamed through southern Italy. We do not know for certain what he wanted. One account says he hoped to lead his people out of Italy over the Alps and home. Other accounts say they wished to stay and plunder. Either way, Roman landowners were terrified.

📚 The End of the Revolt

In 71 BC, the Senate gave command to CrassusMAR-kus lih-SIN-ee-us KRASS-usThe richest man in Rome and the general who beat Spartacus. (Marcus Licinius Crassus), who was said to be the richest man in Rome. He had earned his money in real estate and by buying up property cheaply, and he had fought for Sulla. Crassus took eight legions. He trapped the rebels in the toe of Italy, and in a final battle Spartacus was killed. About six thousand captured slaves were nailed to crosses along the road from Capua to Rome. Many Romans must have seen those crosses, and they were meant as a warning.

The revolt was over, but Rome's problem was not. Slaves did the work on the great estates, and their owners lived in fear of them. Cruelty made the fear greater, and no one in the Senate could see a way to end it. A system built on slavery had shown how fragile it was.

📚 Pompey, the Man of the Hour

Another general claimed credit for finishing off some of the runaways. He was PompeyNEE-us pom-PAY-us MAG-nusThe popular Roman general who won wars in the East. (Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus). Pompey had raised his own army as a young man and fought for Sulla. He won so many victories that he was called "the Great." He was elected consul in 70 BC, even though he was too young and had never held the lower offices. The Romans were willing to bend the rules for him.

In 67 BC, pirates had grown so strong that they cut off Rome's grain ships. The people gave Pompey a special command over the entire Mediterranean. In about three months, he swept the pirates from the sea. Next, he was sent to the east to fight King Mithridates. Pompey defeated him, took Syria, and in 63 BC marched into Jerusalem. By the time he came home, he had added great lands to Rome and stood higher than any other Roman. The Senate began to fear him. A man who could clear the sea and conquer kingdoms might not be willing to go back to being an ordinary citizen. The old rule was that a general gave up his command and his army when a war ended. Pompey, like Sulla before him, made many Romans wonder whether that rule still held.

📚 Cicero and the Plot

Not everyone in Rome relied on a sword. CiceroMAR-kus TUL-ee-us SIS-er-ohRome's greatest speaker, who was consul in 63 BC. (Marcus Tullius Cicero) was a lawyer from a small Italian town, and like Marius, he was a new man. His speeches were so powerful that voters made him consul in 63 BC.

That year, a nobleman named CatilineLOO-shus SER-jee-us kat-ih-LY-nuhThe noble who plotted to seize Rome by force. (Lucius Sergius Catilina) was deeply in debt and angry that he had lost the election. He joined a conspiracykun-SPEER-uh-seeA secret plan by a group of people to do something harmful or illegal. to seize Rome. Cicero learned of the plot and gave speeches in the Senate to expose it. The leaders were arrested, and the Senate had them put to death without a trial. Cicero was hailed as the savior of the Republic. But some Romans said he had broken the law by executing citizens without a trial. A few years later, his enemies drove him into exile for it. The episode shows how easily Romans now set the rules aside when they felt afraid.

📚 Three Men Make a Deal

When Pompey came home, the Senate would not give his veteransVET-er-unA soldier who has served for many years and has left the army. the land they had been promised. Another rising politician, Julius CaesarGY-us JOOL-yus SEE-zerThe ambitious Roman politician and general who later conquered Gaul. (Gaius Julius Caesar), also found the Senate in his way. He came from an old but not very rich family, and he was clever, charming, and deeply in debt. Crassus wanted help for his friends too.

In 60 BC, the three made a private pact. They agreed to support one another, and together they could win any office or law they wanted. We call this the First triumviratetry-UM-vur-itA group of three men who share power., though it was never an official office. It was only a secret deal. Caesar was elected consul in 59 BC, and he passed the laws the other two wanted. Pompey married Caesar's daughter Julia, which tied them together.

This arrangement mattered because the Senate had been bypassed. The Republic's offices still existed, but real decisions were now made by three men in private. Each one needed the other two, and none of them could be sure the pact would last.

💪 KEEP THIS IN MIND

Spartacus showed how cruel and fragile the slave economy was. Pompey, Crassus, and Caesar showed how a few men could outweigh the Senate. Each man needed the others for now, but none of them would stay content to share for long.
