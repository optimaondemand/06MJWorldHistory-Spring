📚 Why Gaul?

We know most of what happened in Gaul because Caesar wrote it down himself. He wrote in plain Latin, and he called himself "Caesar" as if a neutral reporter were speaking. What do we do with a witness who is also the general? Read this lesson the way a detective would: ask what the story says, who wrote it, and what he might have left out.

After his year as consul, CaesarGY-us JOOL-yus SEE-zerThe Roman general who conquered Gaul. needed a province. A governor had an army, a chance for glory, and a way to get rich. Caesar also had huge debts, and a new war could pay them off. He got the governorship of Gaul in 58 BC. Gaul was the land north of Italy and west of the Rhine River. Its people, the Gauls, were divided into many tribes who often fought one another.

Caesar's plan was simple. It was also a gamble, since a failure in Gaul could have ruined him. He would win victories, earn fame, and build an army loyal to him alone. He had seen how Sulla and Pompey rose, and he meant to do the same.

📚 The Gallic War

In 58 BC, the Helvetianshel-VEE-shee-eyeA Gallic people from what is now Switzerland who tried to move west. (Helvetii), a people from what is now Switzerland, tried to move west through Roman land. Caesar stopped them with a quick march and a hard fight. He then drove back a German chief who was pushing into Gaul. Over the next years, he marched north and fought the tribes of Belgium.

In 55 BC, Caesar wanted to show Rome's reach. His engineers built a bridge across the Rhine in ten days, and his army crossed to the German side. They stayed only a short time and came back. Why bother with such shows? Because Caesar knew that Rome loved a daring story. Each bold deed made him more famous at home, where his rivals could not match it. Caesar also sailed across the sea to Britain, which no Roman army had ever reached. He made two landings, in 55 and 54 BC. They did not bring much, but they made great news in Rome.

📚 Caesar's Own Story

We know most of this from Caesar himself. He wrote short books called the Commentarieskom-en-TAH-ree-eyeCaesar's own short books about his wars. (Commentarii), and sent them to Rome each year. He wrote in clear, plain Latin, and he talked about himself in the third person, as "Caesar." This made it sound as if a neutral reporter were describing the facts.

But think about who wrote them. Caesar was a politician who needed support at home. His books made him look wise, brave, and just. He described his enemies as treacherous and his own acts as necessary. This does not mean he told lies all the time. It means we should read him as a source with a point of view, and ask what he wanted Romans to believe.

📚 Vercingetorix and Alesia

For several years, the Gallic tribes had failed to work together. In 52 BC, a young chief named Vercingetorixver-sin-JET-uh-rixThe young Gallic chief who united the tribes against Caesar. changed that. He united many tribes against Rome. He burned villages so the Romans could not find food, and he beat Caesar once in a battle.

Then Vercingetorix made a stand at the hilltop fortress of Alesia. Caesar surrounded it with a siegeSEEJSurrounding a city or fort to cut it off until it gives in.. His soldiers dug long ditches and built a rampartRAM-partA wall of earth or stone built to defend a place. with towers around the whole hill, to keep the Gauls inside. Then, when he heard a huge Gallic army was coming to rescue them, he built a second wall facing outward. Caesar's men were trapped between two armies, and it was a matter of life and death. After days of hard fighting, the Romans held. Vercingetorix gave himself up. He was held in Rome for six years and then killed after Caesar's victory parade.

Some ancient writers claim that a million Gauls died in the war and another million were enslaved. Those numbers are very likely too high, but the war was undoubtedly terrible.

📚 A Disaster in the East

While Caesar fought in the west, Crassus wanted his own glory. In 53 BC, he led an army east against the Parthians. They were a Persian-speaking people who ruled the old Persian lands. Their horse archers rode circles around the Roman foot soldiers and shot them down. At Carrhae, in the desert, the Roman army was destroyed. Crassus was killed.

Caesar's daughter, Julia, who was Pompey's wife, had also died in 54 BC. Now two of the ties that had held the triumviratetry-UM-vur-itA group of three men who share power. together were gone. Only Pompey and Caesar remained, and each began to look at the other with suspicion. Pompey stayed near Rome and the Senate leaned toward him. Caesar was far away with an army. Each man could imagine the other turning on him.

📚 The Gift and the Danger

By about 50 BC, Gaul was conquered. In time, it would become a Roman land. Roads and towns were built, and the Gauls adopted Latin. The French language still comes from that Latin.

But the war's biggest result may have been what it did to Caesar's army. His soldiers had fought with him for years, and they had grown rich. They were loyal to him, not to the Senate. A veteranVET-er-unA soldier who has served for many years and has left the army. of those campaigns would follow Caesar anywhere. For the Republic, this was the danger. The man who commanded the most loyal army in Rome was also the one with the most to fear from his enemies.
